# Any level, any time: the research (31 Oct 2026)

The evidence behind [any-level-any-time.md](any-level-any-time.md), which holds the proposal and
its decisions. Gathered by web research on 31 Oct 2026 in three parts: shorter sessions, people
starting from a low base (older, untrained, with obesity), and placing a person on the ladders.
Every source gives its link and how far it was read (full text, abstract, or a secondary
summary); a figure marked **(derived)** is arithmetic on a published model that no paper
reports. Secondary and abstract-only figures should be checked before the app quotes them.

Time efficiency, the volume bands and the paired-set rests are also in
[program-builder-research.md](program-builder-research.md) (Time efficiency); Part 1 here adds
the dose at low volumes, proximity to failure and the budgets.

## Part 1: Time-efficient hypertrophy training: evidence for shorter sessions

Purpose: the evidence for fitting people with about 40, 30 or 20 minutes of work a session.

**How far each source was read**, given on every citation:
- **[FULL]**: full text read (or the relevant sections searched in the full text).
- **[ABS]**: abstract or database record only.
- **[SEC]**: secondary summary only (search-engine digest, blog, press release). Treat these as unverified.

**Derived figures.** Numbers marked **(derived)** are my own arithmetic from a published model or table. No paper reports them.

### 1. Dose-response at low weekly volumes

#### Sources

**Pelland, Remmert, Robinson, Hinson, Zourdos. Resistance-training dose-response meta-regression (volume and frequency).**
- Published in *Sports Medicine* 2026;56(2):481-505, doi 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. I read the SportRxiv preprint, last modified September 2024, in full: https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/460/967/908 [FULL, preprint]. The published version was abstract-level only and its wording matches: https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139 [ABS].
- Scope: 67 studies, 2,058 participants. Mean age about 25. 28 studies were untrained and 39 trained. 78% of hypertrophy effects used some failure definition. Indirect sets were counted as 0.5 ("fractional"), and this method fit the data best.
- Hypertrophy: the best-fit model was a **square-root** curve. The posterior probability that volume helps was 100%. Returns diminish, with **no clear plateau** up to the ~25+ sets where data thin out.
- The marginal slope at the mean volume (12.25 fractional sets) was **+0.24 % muscle size per extra weekly set** (95% CrI 0.15 to 0.33).
- Strength: the best-fit model was a **reciprocal** curve, with "strong diminishing returns and a functional plateau".
- Frequency had little effect on hypertrophy once volume was accounted for: 91.3% probability of a positive slope, interval compatible with zero. For strength, frequency helped with diminishing returns: about +12.7% at 1 session a week vs +17.3% at 2 sessions.
- The efficiency tiers (Table 2A/2B, verbatim) are below. SDES is the smallest detectable effect size: 2.05% for hypertrophy and 3.96% for strength.

| Outcome | Tier | Fractional sets/wk | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy | Minimum effective dose | **4** | Detectable growth |
| | Higher efficiency | 5-10 | ~6 extra sets per further detectable step |
| | Intermediate | 11-18 | ~8.5 extra sets per step |
| | Lower | 19-29 | ~10.75 extra sets per step |
| | Lowest | 30-42 | ~12.5 extra sets per step |
| Strength | Minimum effective dose | **1** | Detectable strength gain |
| | Higher efficiency | 2 | ~0.75 extra sets per step |
| | Intermediate | 3-4 | ~2.25 extra sets per step |
| | Lower | 5+ | No consistent gain above SDES |

- The authors caution that the strength minimum dose is probably inflated by learning a novel test. In powerlifters, 1-3 direct sets a week were not enough and 3-9 were.

**Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger 2017, *J Sports Sci* 35:1073-82. Weekly volume meta-analysis.**
- Read via the Iversen 2021 full text, which quotes it [SEC via FULL review]. The original was not retrieved: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/
- Hypertrophy was **~5% for <5 sets, ~7% for 5-9 sets and ~10% for 10+ weekly sets per muscle**. A blog by the authors gives 5.4 / 6.6 / 9.8% [SEC].

**Krieger 2009/2010 meta-analyses (single vs multiple sets).**
- Read via Iversen [SEC via FULL review].
- Multiple sets gave **46% larger strength gains and 40% larger hypertrophy** than single sets. Single-set effect sizes were 0.54 (strength) and 0.25 (hypertrophy).

**Baz-Valle et al. 2022, *J Hum Kinet* 81:199-210.**
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/ [FULL, via fetch summary]
- Young trained men; 6 studies in the meta-analysis.
- **12-20 vs >20 weekly sets: no difference** for quadriceps (SMD -0.2) or biceps (-0.1). Triceps favoured >20 (SMD -0.5), but the reported CI looks internally inconsistent.
- Groups under 12 sets were only synthesised qualitatively. Authors suggest 12-20 sets/wk for young trained men. **Weak evidence** for anything above 20.

**Schoenfeld et al. 2019, *MSSE* 51:94-103 (1 vs 3 vs 5 sets per exercise, trained men, 3x/wk, 8 wk).**
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/ [ABS]
- **Strength and endurance gains were similar in all groups.** Hypertrophy followed a dose-response.
- The low-volume group trained about **13 minutes per session**.
- The abstract does not give per-muscle weekly set counts. Seven whole-body exercises overlap muscles, so I could not verify them.

**Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld, Fimland 2021, "No Time to Lift?", *Sports Med* 51:2079-95.**
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772/ [FULL, first 100k characters, which contains all of the body text]
- Recommends **"a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group using a 6-15 RM loading range"**, bilateral multi-joint exercises, and "weekly training volume is more important than training frequency".
- It also cites these maintenance and minimum-dose data:
  - **Bickel et al.**: after 16 weeks of training, **1 or 3 weekly sets per exercise maintained 1RM for 32 weeks**. Size was maintained in young men but not in older men.
  - **Androulakis-Korakakis et al.**: the minimum effective dose for 1RM in trained men was **1 set of 6-12 reps at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3x/week**.
  - In untrained people, 1 set was about equal to 3 sets for the upper body, but 3 sets were better for the legs (Rønnestad).

**Fyfe, Hamilton, Daly 2022, "Minimal-dose resistance training", *Sports Med* 52:463-79.**
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34822137/ [ABS]
- Low session volume works for strength and function in younger and older adults, either as heavier loads at low frequency or as lighter "exercise snacks" at higher frequency.
- Strength and power decline up to 8x faster than muscle mass with age, so for healthspan they argue strength should be prioritised over size.

**ACSM 2026 Position Stand, "Resistance training prescription for muscle function, hypertrophy, and physical performance in healthy adults: an overview of reviews", *MSSE* April 2026.**
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823 [FULL, first 100k of 248k characters]
- Also read the ACSM infographic: https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf [FULL]
- Scope: 137 systematic reviews, more than 30,000 participants.
- Hypertrophy: "≥10 sets/wk (dose-response)". Strength: **2-3 sets per exercise** at ≥80% 1RM and **≥2 sessions/wk**.
- In the Discussion, one set beats zero and two sets beat one, so they advise at least 2 sets per exercise. They cite meta-regressions putting the plateau near 2-3 sets per exercise for strength and around 18-20 weekly sets for hypertrophy.
- "The biggest jump in results happens when you go from no resistance training to any resistance training."

#### Answer: is there a curve that lets the app say "half the sets gives about X% of the result"?

**Yes, roughly. The best single source is Pelland 2026.**
- The hypertrophy curve is square-root shaped and control-adjusted to zero at zero sets. If gain is proportional to √sets, then gain at V/2 ≈ 0.71 × gain at V **(derived)**. That gives these ratios **(derived, model arithmetic only)**:

| Weekly sets | Share of hypertrophy gain at 12 sets | Share at 18 sets |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | ~58% | ~47% |
| 6 | ~71% | ~58% |
| 9 | ~87% | ~71% |
| 12 | 100% | ~82% |

- **Going from 3 rounds to 2 rounds keeps about √(2/3) ≈ 82% of the hypertrophy gain (derived).**
- The categorical data in Schoenfeld 2017 roughly agree. Under 5 sets gives ~5% against ~10% for 10+, so about **50-55%**. 5-9 sets gives about **67-70%** of the 10+ result.
- **Strength barely loses anything.** Pelland's strength curve flattens after about 4-5 fractional sets a week. Schoenfeld 2019 found 1 set per exercise (13-minute sessions) produced the same 1RM gains as 5 sets.
- **Caveats:**
  - These are average curves, mostly from untrained young adults or young trained men in studies of about 10 weeks.
  - Individuals vary widely. Robinson et al. cite Damas: some people grew more on fewer than 10 sets.
  - Credible intervals widen above about 25 sets.
  - The square-root shape is the best fit but is "still compatible with multiple functional forms" (Pelland).
  - **Do not show users exact percentages as fact. Present them as approximate bands.**

### 2. Proximity to failure

#### Sources

**Robinson, Pelland, Remmert, Refalo, Jukic, Steele, Zourdos 2024, *Sports Med* 54(9):2209-31.**
- Read the preprint (November 2023) in full: https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654 [FULL, preprint]. Published abstract: https://x-mol.com/paper/1810175950090465280 [ABS].
- Hypertrophy: 26 studies, 140 effects. **Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure.** The best fit was linear.
  - Standardised slope: -0.019 per RIR (95% CI -0.035 to -0.004).
  - Response-ratio slope: **about -0.48 % muscle size per extra RIR** (95% CI -0.78 to -0.18).
- **Strength: no relationship with RIR** (CIs include zero).
- Only volume-equated studies were included. The authors note that results "may change if participants could modify the number of sets", because failure training causes more fatigue and fewer achievable sets. They call the analysis exploratory, since RIR had to be *estimated* for each study.

**Refalo, Helms, Trexler, Hamilton, Fyfe 2023, *Sports Med* 53:649-65. Failure vs non-failure meta-analysis, 15 studies.**
- [SEC/ABS] https://bibbase.org/network/publication/refalo-helms-trexler-hamilton-fyfe-influenceofresistancetrainingproximitytofailureonskeletalmusclehypertrophyasystematicreviewwithmetaanalysis-2023
- The pooled effect favoured failure only **trivially (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00-0.37)**. The authors suggest the relationship may be non-linear.

**Refalo et al. 2024, *J Sports Sci*. Within-subject trial in trained men and women (n=18), 8 weeks, leg press and leg extension.**
- Compared failure with 1-2 RIR. Abstract via https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Similar-muscle-hypertrophy-following-eight-weeks.pdf [ABS/SEC].
- **Quadriceps growth was essentially the same: 0.181 cm with failure vs 0.182 cm at 1-2 RIR.** Failure caused larger losses in velocity and reps.

**Refalo et al. 2023, *Sports Med Open*. Acute fatigue: failure vs 1-RIR vs 3-RIR, bench press, 24 trained people.**
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9908800 [ABS via search digest]
- Velocity loss right after the session was **-25% at failure, -13% at 1-RIR and -8% at 3-RIR**. At 24 h it was -3% after failure and after 1-RIR, and +2% after 3-RIR. Differences were gone by 48 h.
- Discomfort, exertion and soreness rose linearly as sets got closer to failure.

**Grgic, Schoenfeld, Orazem, Sabol 2022, *J Sport Health Sci* 11:202-11. 15 studies, young adults only.**
- https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662 [ABS/SEC]
- No overall difference for strength (ES -0.09) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22, CI crosses 0).
- **In trained participants, failure favoured hypertrophy (ES 0.15, 95% CI 0.03-0.26).** When volume was not equated, non-failure was better for strength (ES -0.32).

**Lasevicius et al. 2022, *JSCR* 36:346-51. Untrained men, unilateral knee extension.**
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/ [ABS/SEC]
- **With low loads, failure mattered:** CSA rose 7.8% at failure vs 2.8% (not significant) when stopped short. **With high loads it did not:** 8.1% vs 7.7%.
- This is directly relevant to high-rep bodyweight sets.

**Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, *Eur J Appl Physiol*. 10 trained men.**
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28965198/ [ABS]
- Recovery after 3x10 to failure was slower than after non-failure protocols, with significant differences at **24-48 h**.

**Older adults.**
- Bergamasco et al. 2022, *JSCR*, ages 60-77, 40% 1RM [SEC]: failure, voluntary stop and fixed 10 reps **all gave similar strength gains**. No hypertrophy was detected in any arm. https://repositorio.ufscar.br/handle/ufscar/11700
- ACSM 2026 [FULL, part]: failure "does not enhance gains" on average, and it "may be inadvisable for some older adults due to vascular and injury risks". Suggested target is near failure, about 2-3 RIR.

#### Answer
- **Training to failure does not substitute for missing sets.**
  - Robinson's slope (~0.5% extra growth per RIR closer to failure) comes from volume-equated designs. Going from 2 RIR to 0 RIR buys roughly 1 percentage point of extra growth over a typical ~10-week study **(derived)**.
  - Pelland's slope is ~0.24% per extra set at mean volume, so that is on the order of 4 extra sets. **This comparison is very rough:** different datasets, estimated RIR, and wide intervals.
  - The direct trial in trained people (Refalo 2024) found **no difference between 0 and 1-2 RIR**. Failure costs more fatigue: about double the acute velocity loss, and 24-48 h slower recovery.
- **Beginners vs trained:** a small benefit of failure appears only in trained subgroups (Grgic 2022 ES 0.15). For beginners, stopping at 1-3 RIR is enough. Their RIR estimates are also poor, so some guided "find failure once" sets help calibration (Robinson's discussion notes RIR accuracy improves near failure).
- **Older adults:** no benefit shown, and ACSM flags vascular and injury concerns. Keep them at 1-3 RIR.
- **Load matters:** failure (or 0-1 RIR) matters most for **low-load, high-rep sets** (Lasevicius 2022). This covers bodyweight push-ups, squats and inverted rows done for 20+ reps. With heavier loads (6-15 RM) it adds little.
- **Risk on compound bodyweight moves.** I found **no trial measuring injury from failure on compound vs isolation exercises. This is expert opinion only.** The commonly cited concern is breakdown of technique (lumbar flexion in hinges, kipping or partial reps on pull-ups, sagging hips in push-ups). Taking a set to failure on curls, lateral raises, leg extensions or machines carries less consequence if form slips. Treat this as a reasonable design rule, not a finding.

### 3. Time-saving techniques

| Technique | Evidence | Time saved | Outcome per set | Source / depth |

**Answer.** Three techniques cut session time without losing outcome per set:
1. **Pairing non-competing or antagonist exercises.** The evidence is strongest here, and it is what the app already does.
2. **Drop sets.**
3. **Rest-pause.**

The cost of all three is higher perceived effort, with somewhat more muscle damage and fatigue the next day (Weakley 2017; Zhang 2025; Havers 2026).

Rest between sets for the *same* muscle should stay at **≥60 s, ideally about 90 s or more** (Singer 2024). Pairing gets this for free: in the app's A→15 s→B→60 s scheme, muscle A rests for B's set plus 75 s, which is about 1.5-2 min **(derived)**. Pairs that share a prime mover behave like "similar biomechanical supersets", which cost volume load (Zhang SMD -1.08). The app's 90 s rule for those pairs is therefore justified, and 90-120 s would be safer.

### 4. Practical session templates by time budget

**What the literature actually gives:**
- **Iversen 2021** [FULL]:
  - At least 4 weekly sets per muscle at 6-15 RM.
  - As few as 3 multi-joint exercises (a leg press or squat, a pull, a push) can cover the major muscles.
  - Use supersets, drop sets or rest-pause to halve the time.
  - Warm up specifically, only for the first exercise of each muscle.
  - It gives no minute-by-minute template.
- **Schoenfeld 2019** [ABS]: 13-minute whole-body sessions (7 exercises × 1 set, 3x/wk) gave the same strength gains as 5 sets and less hypertrophy.
- **ACSM 2026** [FULL, part]: all major muscle groups ≥2 days/wk, ~10 sets/muscle/wk for hypertrophy, 2-3 sets per exercise for strength. "Start here: train all major muscle groups at least 2 days per week."
- **Momma et al. 2022**, *BJSM* 56:755, cohort meta-analysis, observational [ABS/SEC], https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/13/755: the largest reduction in all-cause mortality, CVD and cancer risk came at **about 30-60 minutes a week** of muscle-strengthening activity, with a J-shaped curve.
- **NSCA:** I found no NSCA time-budget template, only a secondary mention of 30-90 s rest for hypertrophy. **Not verified.**

#### Throughput model (derived)
- Assume a working set of about 40 s, then A → 15 s → B → 60 s. One paired round is about 40+15+40+60 = 155 s for 2 sets, so **about 1.3 min per hard set**.
- This matches Weakley's superset condition: 24 min for 18 sets = 1.33 min per set.
- Sessions then hold about **31 sets in 40 min, 23 in 30 min and 15 in 20 min** of work **(derived)**.

#### Weekly sets available and what they buy per muscle (derived)
The allocation assumes about 6-7 muscle groups (quads, posterior chain, chest/push, back/pull, delts, arms, calves/core). Compound lifts add 0.5 fractional sets to synergists.

| Weekly budget | Total hard sets/wk | Fractional sets per main muscle (approx.) | Tier (Pelland) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 × 50-60 min (current) | ~115-140 | 14-20 | Intermediate efficiency, full target |
| **3 × 40 min** | ~93 | **10-14** | Meets the 10-20 target at its low end |
| **2 × 40 min** | ~62 | **7-10** | "Higher efficiency" tier. Hypertrophy roughly 75-90% of a 12-set week (derived). Strength essentially full |
| **3 × 30 min** | ~69 | **8-11** | Similar to 2 × 40 but with more frequency (better for strength) |
| 2 × 30 min | ~46 | 5-7 | Above minimum effective dose. Good maintenance |
| 3 × 20 min | ~45 | 5-7 | Above minimum effective dose |
| 2 × 20 min | ~30 | ~4 | At the minimum effective dose (Iversen ≥4; Pelland 4) |

### 5. Warm-up minimum

- **Iversen 2021** [FULL]: "Restrict the warm-up to exercise-specific warm-ups." Limit it to the first exercise for each muscle, and use it particularly for loads above 80% 1RM. Skip the general warm-up when time is short.
  - It cites **Ribeiro et al.**: in recreationally trained men doing 3 sets at 80% 1RM, neither a 10-min ergometer warm-up nor 10 reps at 50% changed total reps or fatigue.
- **Ribeiro AS et al. 2014**, *Percept Mot Skills*, 15 men, sets to failure at 80% 1RM [SEC], https://www.scribd.com/document/362423660/ribeiro2014-aquecimen-pdf: specific, aerobic and combined warm-ups produced **no difference** in total reps or fatigue index compared with no warm-up.
- **Ribeiro B et al. 2021**, *Motricidade*, systematic review of 11 studies [ABS/SEC], https://revistas.rcaap.pt/motricidade/article/view/21143: results on general plus specific warm-up were "not consensual". Specific warm-up near the working load helped maximal strength in some studies.
- **Ribeiro B et al. 2021**, two crossover trials with 34 and 40 trained men [SEC]: a progressive specific warm-up (2×6 at 40% and 80% of the training load) improved first-set velocity and force. A few light reps alone were "not enough".
- **Neves et al. 2025**, scoping review [ABS], https://journal.hep.com.cn/josisae/EN/10.1007/s42978-025-00361-9: the effect of general warm-up on strength measures is still unclear.
- **ACSM 2026:** does not address warm-up.
- **Gap:** I found no resistance-training-specific evidence that a general warm-up prevents injury. Injury-prevention warm-up evidence comes from team sports and was out of scope here.

**Answer:**
- For hypertrophy work at moderate loads (6-15 RM, 1-2 RIR), **a separate general warm-up is not needed for performance**.
- **One or two ramp sets of the first exercise per movement pattern** can replace it. Bodyweight example: an easier progression for about 8-10 reps at roughly 4+ RIR.
- A short general warm-up (2-3 min) can stay as a comfort or adherence choice. Cold environments and older users are reasonable reasons. The evidence does not require it.

### 6. Concurrent training (cardio + resistance on a short budget)

- **WHO 2020** (Bull et al., *BJSM* 54:1451), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/ [FULL, Table 4]:
  - Adults should do **150-300 min/wk moderate, or 75-150 min/wk vigorous** aerobic activity, or an equivalent mix (1 vigorous minute ≈ 2 moderate minutes).
  - **Plus** muscle-strengthening "involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week".
  - Adults 65+ should also do multicomponent balance and strength work **≥3 days/wk**.
  - The 10-minute minimum bout was dropped in 2020; all activity counts.
- **Schumann et al. 2022**, *Sports Med* 52:601-12, concurrent vs resistance training alone [ABS], https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/:
  - **No interference for hypertrophy (SMD -0.01) or maximal strength (-0.06).**
  - Explosive strength was attenuated (-0.28), **especially when both are done in the same session**. No significant attenuation with ≥3 h between them.
- **Lundberg, Feuerbacher, Sünkeler, Schumann 2022**, *Sports Med* 52:2391-403, fibre-level [FULL via fetch digest], https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354/:
  - Small possible reduction in **fibre** hypertrophy (SMD -0.23, p=0.05).
  - The interference in type I fibres was driven by **running** (SMD -0.81, only 3 studies). **Cycling showed no interference.**
  - Same-day vs separate-day made no significant difference.
- **ACSM 2026:** concurrent effects on strength and hypertrophy "cannot determine". No recommendation.
- **Storoschuk, Moran-MacDonald, Gibala, Gurd 2025**, "Much Ado About Zone 2", *Sports Med* 55:1611-24, narrative review [ABS], https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/:
  - Evidence "does not support Zone 2 training as the optimal intensity" for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation capacity.
  - "Prioritizing higher exercise intensities (> Zone 2) is critical" for cardiometabolic health, especially at low training volumes.
- **Gillen et al. 2016**, *PLOS One*, sedentary men, small n (9/10/6) [SEC/ABS], https://doaj.org/article/0d8f38860885479c87ee1ba61460002b:
  - 3 × 20 s all-out sprints, about 10 min per session including warm-up and cool-down.
  - Gave the same ~19% VO2peak gain and insulin-sensitivity improvement as about 50 min of moderate continuous cycling.
- **No evidence found** for a specific "Zone 2 minimum". The claims circulating online are mostly from blogs [SEC]. **Treat any Zone 2 minimum as unsupported.**

**Answer:**
- With limited time, resistance and aerobic training do not meaningfully compete for hypertrophy or maximal strength.
- Lifting first and cardio after (or on another day) protects the lifting quality.
- Cycling, rowing or brisk walking interfere less than running at the fibre level, on preliminary evidence.
- Covering WHO's aerobic minimum takes either 150 min/wk moderate, or 75 min/wk vigorous. Vigorous work halves the time; intervals compress it further, with weaker evidence for the 10-minute sprint protocols.

### Implications for an app (concrete rules)

Each rule below is tagged with its source. "(derived)" means my arithmetic on a published model. Rules marked *design choice* rest on expert opinion or extrapolation, not trial data.

#### A. Volume floors and targets per muscle per week (fractional sets: indirect = 0.5)

| Rule | Threshold | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | **Hard floor: 4 fractional sets/muscle/wk.** Below this, hypertrophy is not detectably above zero. Label it "minimum effective dose". | Pelland 2026 Table 2A; Iversen 2021 |
| A2 | **Strength floor: about 2-3 sets per main lift per week, on ≥2 days.** Strength plateaus around 4-5 fractional sets. | Pelland 2026 Table 2B; ACSM 2026; Schoenfeld 2019 |
| A3 | **"Good" band: 8-12 sets.** This is where marginal returns are still high (~6 extra sets per detectable step in the 5-10 range). **"Full" band: 10-20 sets**, which keeps the existing target. | Pelland 2026; ACSM 2026 (≥10); Baz-Valle 2022 (12-20, trained men) |
| A4 | **Maintenance mode:** about 1/3 of normal volume (e.g. 1 set per exercise per week) holds strength for months. Older users need more to hold size. | Bickel via Iversen 2021 |
| A5 | When reducing rounds, show **approximate shares, not exact ones**: 3→2 rounds keeps about 80% of the hypertrophy result and about all of the strength result. 12→6 sets keeps about 70%. 12→4 keeps about 55-60%. Phrase as "most of the result". | (derived) Pelland 2026 square-root model; cross-check Schoenfeld 2017 categories (5/7/10%) |

#### B. Time budgets (session work time, excluding warm-up)
Throughput is about 1.3 min per hard set with paired sets (derived from the app's 15 s/60 s scheme; matches Weakley 2017).

| Budget | Sets/session | Weekly structure that hits the floor | Expected tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **3 × 40 min** | ~30 | Full body ×3, each main muscle 3-5 sets/session | 10-14 sets/muscle: full target |
| **2 × 40 min** | ~30 | Full body ×2, each main muscle 4-5 sets/session; arms and calves via fractional sets from compounds plus 1-2 direct sets | 7-10 sets: "good" band |
| **3 × 30 min** | ~23 | Full body ×3, 3 sets per main muscle per session | 8-11 sets: "good" band, better for strength (frequency) |
| 2 × 30 min | ~23 | Full body ×2, compound-only (squat, hinge, push, pull, plus 1 accessory pair) | 5-7 sets: above the floor |
| 3 × 20 min | ~15 | Full body ×3, 2 sets per compound pattern | 5-7 sets: above the floor |
| 2 × 20 min | ~15 | Compounds only | About 4: at the floor. Warn the user that this is maintenance or minimum |

- B1 *(design choice from Iversen 2021)*: **Below 30 min, drop single-joint accessories first.** Keep bilateral multi-joint moves, and count their synergist credit.
- B2 *(design choice from Pelland and ACSM frequency findings)*: **Prefer 3 shorter sessions over 2 longer ones at equal weekly time** when strength matters. For hypertrophy it is neutral.
- B3 (Ramos-Campo 2024): **Full-body vs split does not matter at equal volume.** Below 3 × 40, default to full-body so every muscle gets ≥2 exposures a week (WHO, ACSM).

#### C. Rest by pairing

| Rule | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Non-competing / antagonist pairs: keep 15 s switch + 60 s after the pair.** Each muscle effectively rests about 1.5-2 min, inside the window where rest stops mattering (>60-90 s). | Singer 2024; Zhang 2025 (derived timing) |
| C2 | **Pairs sharing a prime mover: 90-120 s** (the current 90 s is the minimum). Same-muscle pairing loses volume load (SMD -1.08). | Zhang 2025 subgroup |
| C3 | **Never give one muscle under 60 s between its own sets.** | Singer 2024 |
| C4 | Tri-sets or circuits of 3 non-competing moves can be used at **≤20 min budgets** (Weakley: 17.7 vs 24 min for the same 18 sets). Expect higher RPE and more next-day fatigue, so skip them before a session that needs quality. | Weakley 2017 |

#### D. Proximity to failure

| Rule | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| D1 | **Default 1-2 RIR stays.** Failure does not reliably replace missing sets, and it roughly doubles acute fatigue. | Refalo 2024; Refalo 2023 (Sports Med Open); Robinson 2024 caveat |
| D2 | **Optional "last set to 0-1 RIR"**, only on **isolation, machine or stable exercises** (curls, lateral raises, push-ups on knees, leg extension), and only for trained users (≥6-12 months). The small failure benefit appears only in trained subgroups (ES 0.15). | Grgic 2022; Robinson 2024. The isolation-only restriction is a *design choice*, as no injury data was found |
| D3 | **High-rep bodyweight sets (above ~20 reps, i.e. light relative load): push to 0-1 RIR.** Low-load sets stopped short grow little. Better still, progress to a harder variation so sets stay at 6-15 reps. | Lasevicius 2022; Iversen 2021 (6-15 RM) |
| D4 | **No failure on compound bodyweight or free-weight moves for beginners or older users.** Use 2-3 RIR for 60+. | ACSM 2026; Bergamasco 2022; *design choice* for compounds |
| D5 | **After a failure-heavy session, avoid hard sets for the same muscles within 48 h.** | Morán-Navarro 2017; Refalo 2023 |

#### E. Drop sets and rest-pause (time compression)

| Rule | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | **Offer drop sets only when the budget is ≤30 min**, and only on the **last set of an isolation or machine-style move**. One drop set ≈ 2-3 normal sets of time at about half to one-third the duration, with similar hypertrophy. Count it as **2 sets (conservative)**. | Sødal 2023; Havers 2026; Fink via Iversen (1 drop set vs 3 sets) |
| E2 | The bodyweight equivalent is a **mechanical drop set** (e.g. decline push-up → push-up → knee push-up). This is an *extrapolation*: no trial found on mechanical drops. | n/a |
| E3 | **Rest-pause** (one set to near failure, 15-20 s, mini-sets) is acceptable for ≤30 min budgets. Supporting evidence is thinner than for drop sets (Prestes: 35 vs 57 min, similar strength). **Myo-reps have no direct trial evidence.** Treat them as rest-pause. | Iversen 2021; ACSM 2026 |
| E4 | **Limit drop sets and rest-pause to about 1-2 per session.** RPE rises sharply (SMD 1.62), which risks adherence. | Havers 2026; Fyfe 2022 (adherence rationale) |

#### F. Warm-up

| Rule | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | **For budgets ≤40 min, replace the 5-min general warm-up with 1-2 ramp sets of the first exercise of each movement pattern**, e.g. an easier variation for 8-10 reps at about 4+ RIR. These count as 0 hard sets. | Iversen 2021; Ribeiro 2014; Ribeiro 2021 review |
| F2 | Keep an optional 2-3 min general warm-up for older users or cold environments. Neither performance data nor RT-specific injury data requires it. | Neves 2025 (unclear general effect) |

#### G. Cardio sharing the budget

| Rule | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| G1 | **Lift first, cardio after, or on a separate day (≥3 h apart).** Hypertrophy and maximal strength are unaffected. Only explosive strength suffers when both are in one session. | Schumann 2022 |
| G2 | **Prefer cycling, rowing or walking over running** for users focused on hypertrophy. This rests on preliminary fibre-level data. | Lundberg 2022 |
| G3 | Total weekly guidance: **150-300 min moderate or 75-150 min vigorous aerobic, plus strength ≥2 days for all major muscles.** Add balance or multicomponent work ≥3 days for 65+. Short of time: **use vigorous minutes**, which count double. | WHO 2020 |
| G4 | **Do not hard-code a "Zone 2 minimum"**: unsupported. Low-volume, higher-intensity cardio is the time-efficient default. | Storoschuk 2025; Gillen 2016 |
| G5 | On health grounds alone, **about 30-60 min/wk of strength training captures most of the mortality-risk benefit** (observational). Even 2 × 20-30 min is worthwhile for health; the extra time is about muscle and strength goals. | Momma 2022 |

#### Evidence quality flags (for the proposal)
- **Strong** (several meta-analyses agree): volume dose-response with diminishing returns; frequency irrelevant for hypertrophy at equal volume; supersets save time without losing adaptation; failure not necessary on average; concurrent training does not blunt hypertrophy.
- **Moderate:** exact minimum effective doses (4 sets for hypertrophy, 1-4 for strength); rest >60 s small benefit; drop-set equivalence (small studies).
- **Weak or none:** percentage-of-result figures (derived only); myo-reps; mechanical drop sets; lengthened partials as a time saver; injury risk of failure on compound vs isolation moves; general warm-up for RT injury prevention; any Zone 2 minimum; older-adult dose-response (Pelland's sample was mean age 25).

### 1. Older adults and frail people: resistance, power and balance guidelines

#### ICFSR International Exercise Recommendations in Older Adults (Izquierdo et al., J Nutr Health Aging 2021;25(7):824-853)
URL: https://doi.org/10.1007/s12603-021-1665-8 (PDF mirror read: https://sarkopenija.projektas.vu.lt/pdf/rekomendacijosANGL.pdf). **FULL** for the prescription sections and Tables 1-2.
- **Table 1 (resistance training):**
  - Frequency: 2-3 days/week.
  - Volume: 1-3 sets of 8-12 reps across 8-10 major muscle groups.
  - Intensity: "Start at 30-40% of 1RM and progress to heavier loads of 70-80% 1RM (15-18 on Borg Scale)". Rest 1-3 min between sets.
  - Power training at 40-60% 1RM.
- **Text on resistance training:**
  - Train 2-3 times/week, "starting with 1-2 sets and progressing to 2-3 sets of 8-12 repetitions".
  - 6-10 exercises in total, "broken down into sets of 3-4 exercises for novice trainees".
  - At least one rest day between sessions that train the same muscle group.
- **For frail people, include task-specific exercises** such as sit-to-stand and step-ups. Triceps (elbow extensor) strength is critical for transfers.
- **Load progression in supervised frail settings:** "50, 60, 70, and then 80% of the 1 RM over the first four training days (i.e., 1 to 2 weeks)". After that, raise the load as tolerated while keeping effort at 15-18 on the 6-20 Borg scale.
  - Caution: "even a small injury may require weeks to recover and result in the exercise regimen being abandoned".
  - "Even extreme frailty is not a contraindication to robust exercise."
- **Sequencing for severe frailty:** "standing up requires strength and power, staying upright involves balance, and walking any distance requires endurance". Trying to get people walking who "cannot lift their body weight out of a chair or maintain standing balance is likely to fail and increase risk of falls". The suggested order is strength/power, then balance, then aerobic.
  - For adherence, start sedentary older adults on a single modality before adding others.
- **Power training:**
  - Lift fast, lower slowly, and avoid concentric failure.
  - Bodyweight power progression: rise from a chair slowly with help, then alone, then "as fast as possible, with a slight jump at the end of the chair rise where feasible".
  - Very light, very fast loading "poses a risk of meniscus or tendon injuries" and is not recommended. Screen for rotator cuff and meniscus tears.
  - If movement through range hurts, use isometrics against an immovable object, then pain-free range of motion, then full range.
- **Aerobic:**
  - Start with 5-10 min per session (or less) in the first weeks, progressing to 15-30 min, 3-7 days/week. Sessions can be split into bouts of a few minutes.
  - Intensity 12-14 on the Borg scale.
  - When heart rate is invalid (atrial fibrillation, pacemaker, beta-blockers), use perceived exertion. With cognitive impairment, an observer rates exertion from breathing rate, "ability to talk", sweating and facial expression.
  - With severe balance or arthritis problems, use recumbent cycling, aquatic exercise or seated steppers. Arm ergometry suits people who cannot use their legs.
- **Balance:**
  - 1-7 days/week, 1-2 sets of 4-10 exercises.
  - Progress by narrowing the base of support (from two feet holding a chair back to one foot with no hand support), reducing sensory input (eyes closed, foam), perturbing the centre of mass, and dual-tasking.
  - Practise "the most challenging posture or movement possible without falling in a safe environment". When it is "mastered", move to the next level.
  - Load rule: "as soon as a load no longer feels 'hard' to lift on the perceived exertion scale, it can then be increased".
- **Bone (Table 2):**
  - Resistance training 3 days/week at 70-80% 1RM.
  - Impact work of about 50 jumps per session, "Not recommended for people with vertebral osteoporosis".
  - Balance: practise "the most difficult balance posture not yet mastered".
- The paper names **Vivifrail** as an exemplar programme (see section 5).

#### NSCA position statement: Resistance Training for Older Adults (Fragala et al., JSCR 2019;33(8):2019-2052)
URLs: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/ (**ABSTRACT**). Figures from Kravitz's summary, https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/NSCApositionstand.html (**SECONDARY**). The Japanese translation (nsca-japan.or.jp) downloaded but only the numbers extracted, so the original English PDF was not readable.
- **Healthy older adults** (per the summary):
  - 2-3 sets per major muscle group; beginners may start with single sets.
  - 8-10 exercises, 2-3 days/week.
  - 10-15 reps for beginners, 6-12 for strength.
  - Progress from under 51% 1RM, to 51-69%, and for some to 70-85% 1RM.
  - Training to failure adds no shown benefit.
  - Power: explosive concentric phase at 40-60% 1RM.
- **Frail adults:**
  - 3 sessions/week, building to 3 sets of 8-12.
  - Start at 20-30% 1RM and rise towards 80% 1RM.
  - Power at 30-60% 1RM; include sit-to-stand.
  - Machines are a suitable starting point for frail or beginner clients.
- **Safety:** know the client's cardiovascular risk factors. Unstable medical conditions should see a physician first. Avoid training during acute blood pressure elevation from uncontrolled hypertension. With osteoarthritis, adjust selection, intensity, volume and range of motion.

#### WHO 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour (Bull et al., BJSM 2020;54:1451-62)
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/ (**FULL**, first 100k characters).
- **Adults 65 and over:**
  - "Varied multicomponent physical activity that emphasises functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity on 3 or more days a week".
  - Muscle-strengthening work for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days.
- **Good-practice statements:**
  - "Some physical activity is better than none."
  - "Start with small amounts of physical activity and gradually increase frequency, intensity and duration over time."
  - "Older adults should be as physically active as their functional ability allows."
- **Clearance:** "Pre-exercise medical clearance is generally unnecessary for individuals without contraindications." "Those who develop new symptoms when increasing their levels of activity should consult a healthcare provider."
- **Chronic conditions:** activity "is considered safe for adults living with the selected chronic conditions without contraindications".
- **Pregnancy:** applies to women without contraindications.

#### ACSM 2026 Position Stand: Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults (Currier ... Phillips, MSSE 2026)
URL: PDF copy at https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Prescription-for-Muscl.pdf (**FULL** for Table 6 and the Discussion). ACSM infographic: https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf (**FULL**).
- **Main messages:**
  - "The biggest jump in results happens when you go from no resistance training to any resistance training."
  - "Train all major muscle groups at least 2 days per week and build gradually."
  - Bands, bodyweight and home training are effective.
- **Effort:** sets to failure do "not enhance gains" and failure "may also be inadvisable for certain populations (e.g., older individuals)". A sufficient stimulus is "near-failure" or "2-3 repetitions in reserve". However, "there is insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets."
- **Sets:** "Healthy adults are advised to complete at least two sets per exercise." There are diminishing returns beyond about 2-3 sets per exercise for strength.
- **Muscle groups:** upper/lower × push/pull ("four body regions") is enough to cover the major muscle groups.
- **Safety:** in more than 38,000 participants (over 11,000 of them older adults), exercise "did not increase the risk of serious adverse events". In 23 studies of people with coronary heart disease, the musculoskeletal complications during resistance training "were caused by preexisting conditions (knee arthritis) and were resolved by changing RT intensity or body position".
- **Scope caveat:** the stand applies to *healthy adults*. Guidelines for older adults and clinical groups are left to future work.

#### ACSM 2009 Position Stand: Progression Models in Resistance Training (MSSE 2009;41:687-708)
URL: https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf (**FULL** for the abstract and older-adults section). Superseded by the 2026 stand, but still the only ACSM source for a concrete load-increment rule.
- **Novices:** 8-12RM loads (60-70% 1RM), 2-3 days/week.
- **Progression:** "2-10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number".
- **Older adults:** 1-3 sets, 60-80% 1RM, 8-12 reps, 2-3 days/week. "Caution must be taken ... as to the rate of progression, particularly those with hypertension, arthritis, cardiovascular disease".

#### Falls prevention (Sherrington)
- **Cochrane review 2019, BJSM abridgement 2020:** https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/54/15/885 and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7689963 (**SECONDARY / ABSTRACT** via search).
  - Exercise reduces the rate of falls by 23% (RaR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71-0.83; 59 studies; high certainty).
  - Balance and functional exercise: RaR 0.76.
  - The 2020 WHO-linked update found that programmes of 3 or more hours/week that include balance and functional exercise cut the fall rate by 42% (IRR 0.58).
- **Sherrington et al., BJSM 2017;51:1750:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27707740/ (**ABSTRACT**).
  - Exercise cut falls by 21%. Programmes with a high balance challenge and more than 3 h/week gave a 39% reduction (IRR 0.61).
  - No effect was found in residential care, stroke, or after recent hospital discharge.
  - The derived practice rules (high balance challenge, at least 3 h/week, ongoing) are quoted in a BMC Geriatrics 2020 paper (**SECONDARY**).
- **World Falls Guidelines (Montero-Odasso et al., Age Ageing 2022):** https://doi.org/10.1093/ageing/afac205 (**ABSTRACT**). All older adults should be advised on falls prevention and physical activity; high-risk people should get a multifactorial assessment. I did not verify the exercise dosing.

### 2. People with obesity

#### EASO Physical Activity Working Group (Oppert et al., Obesity Reviews 2021;22(S4):e13273)
URL: https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/174925/1/obr.13273.pdf (**FULL** for Table 3 and the practice implications).
- **Weight and fat loss:** "150 to 200 min of aerobic exercise at least at moderate intensity" (Grade A). Tell people "expected weight loss is on average not more than 2 to 3 kg" (Grade A).
- **HIIT:** recommend it "only after thorough assessment of cardiovascular risk and ... with supervision" (Grade B).
- **Weight maintenance:** 200-300 min/week of moderate exercise.
- **Lean mass during weight loss:** resistance training at moderate-to-high intensity (Grade B). Resistance training alone or combined is preferred for muscular fitness.
- **Practice implications:**
  - Programmes should "primarily aim to increase physical fitness, reduce cardiometabolic risk, and improve quality of life", because weight effects are modest.
  - Myocardial infarction and sudden death are most common "in the least habitually physically active individuals performing unaccustomed physical activity". So progress gradually by adjusting duration, frequency and/or intensity.
  - Musculoskeletal injuries are the most frequent side effect. There are no direct comparisons of injury risk by BMI.

#### ESSA position statement: Physical activity in the management of obesity in adults (Johnson et al., JSAMS 2021)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34531124/ (**ABSTRACT**). Table 3 read in full in the Spanish translation at https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/9217002.pdf (**FULL** for Table 3 and the medications section).
- **Volume:**
  - More than 150, ideally 300 min/week of at least moderate activity to prevent gain.
  - 300-420 min/week for meaningful weight loss.
  - The role of resistance training and HIIT for weight is "uncertain".
  - Health benefits occur whatever the weight change.
- **Table 3, severe obesity:** first encourage less sitting and more habitual activity; "consider exercises that may be more tolerable ... including activities while seated and resistance training"; then progress towards weight-management doses.
- **Table 3, musculoskeletal limitation** (for example osteoarthritis):
  - Manage symptoms such as pain first.
  - Strengthening exercises help but don't count towards weekly weight-management minutes.
  - "Aquatic exercise may fall below the recommended (MVPA) intensity"; monitor intensity.
- **Medications:** phentermine can alter heart rate and blood pressure responses, so avoid heart-rate-based prescription.

#### Obesity Canada CPG chapter, Physical Activity in Obesity Management (Boulé and Prud'homme, 2020)
URL: https://obesitycanada.ca/guidelines/physicalactivity (PDF returned 404). **SECONDARY** via search snippets.
- Activity benefits health "across all BMI categories, even in the absence of weight loss".
- The aerobic recommendation is framed around 30-60 min of moderate-to-vigorous activity on most days.
- Brisk walking is the most-prescribed modality.
- I could not verify the grades or the resistance training wording.

#### ACSM 2009 position stand on weight loss (Donnelly et al.)
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19127177/ (**SECONDARY**). 150-250 min/week gives modest loss; more than 250 min/week is associated with clinically significant loss.

#### Joint loading, floor transfers, positions, heat, chafing

**Knee load (Messier et al., Arthritis Rheum 2005;52:2026)**
URL: press release at https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2005-06/jws-tio062805.php (**SECONDARY / ABSTRACT**).
- In 142 older adults with overweight/obesity and knee osteoarthritis, each pound lost meant "a 4-fold reduction in the load exerted on the knee per step".
- Implication: walking and stepping volume loads the knees in proportion to body mass. Non-weight-bearing options (cycling, water, seated work) cut that load.

**Severe obesity review (Lee et al., J Obes Metab Syndr 2025/2026;35(1):1-13)**
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12884621/ (**FULL**, **WEAK**: a qualitative synthesis of 10 studies, and the protocols are not validated).
- Start at low-to-moderate intensity, use stable support before instability, and avoid dumbbells and barbells early.
- It does not cover floor transfers, heat, chafing or breathlessness.

**Practitioner sources on floor work and positions** (**WEAK**, expert or practitioner opinion only)
URLs: https://archive25.nfpt.com/?p=60146 and the sparkpeople and ideafit search snippets.
- Decide whether floor work is needed at all: getting down and up can be hard, embarrassing or unsafe.
- Use seated or standing alternatives, or a raised surface or bench.
- Use an inclined bench (about 45 degrees) instead of lying flat.
- Avoid plyometrics and jumping early.
- **No guideline-grade evidence found** on supine positions with reflux, prone positions, or chafing. Treat these as common-sense comfort adaptations, not evidence-based rules.

**Heat**
- US Army data: exertional heat stroke risk rose about 3% per BMI unit. Military data: OR 1.94 for serious heat illness at BMI 30 or above.
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11219003/ and search summaries (**ABSTRACT / SECONDARY**).
- Mechanism: a lower body-surface-to-mass ratio.
- The populations were young soldiers, so extrapolating to app users is weak. I did not read the 2023 ACSM heat illness consensus.

**Affect and adherence (Ekkekakis et al., Ann Behav Med 2008)**
URL: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12160-008-9025-z (**ABSTRACT**).
- Intensity above the ventilatory threshold consistently reduced pleasure during exercise.
- Women with obesity found HIIT less pleasant than moderate continuous exercise (Decker and Ekkekakis, **SECONDARY**).
- Self-selected intensity may improve how exercise feels (Frontiers 2025, single study, **ABSTRACT**).
- Relevance: a sedentary person with obesity started at HIIT 20 s/10 s is likely to have an unpleasant session and drop out.

**GLP-1 drugs**
- Professional statements converge on "do resistance training to preserve lean mass" but rarely specify a dose.
- Source: ukactive 2025 review, https://www.ukactive.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/GLP-1-medications-and-muscle-mass-preservation.-Implications-and-recommendations.pdf (**SECONDARY**, not read in full).
- A 2025 multi-society nutrition advisory reportedly says resistance training 2-3x/week (**SECONDARY, unverified**).

### 3. Untrained and deconditioned adults

#### How low to start
- ICFSR: start resistance training at 30-40% 1RM with 1-2 sets and 3-4 exercises; start aerobic work at 5-10 min or less.
- NSCA (frail): start at 20-30% 1RM.
- WHO: start small and build gradually.
- ACSM 2026: any resistance training at all is the biggest win.
- PAR-Q+ cleared message: "start slowly and build up gradually".

#### What "level 0" looks like in published programmes
- **Vivifrail Type A** ("cannot rise from a chair or a bed"): walking "will start only when the elderly person has improved their muscle strength".
- **ICFSR:** if someone "cannot support their body weight independently", prioritise strength, power and balance before walking.
- **Chair-based exercise** works for upper and lower body function, including the 30 s chair stand (Klempel et al., IJERPH 2021, 25 studies). Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7920319/ (**ABSTRACT**).
- A JAMDA 2021 nursing-home review reports gains even in residents who cannot walk (**SECONDARY**).

#### Muscle soreness, the repeated-bout effect, and starting below capacity
- **Chen, Nosaka et al.** (several papers 2012-2021; repository records at https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2012/560; **ABSTRACT**):
  - A light eccentric bout at about 10% of maximal strength reduced muscle damage from a later maximal eccentric bout. This held in untrained young men, men aged about 66, and across 9 muscle groups.
  - Protection lasted about 2 weeks and was gone by 3 weeks (except for soreness).
  - Small samples (about 12-13 per group).
- **Implications:**
  - A deliberately easy first week buys protection for the harder weeks that follow.
  - After a gap of 3 weeks or more, assume the protection has gone and step back down.
  - I found **no direct trial** showing that easy starts improve *adherence*. That link is plausible but unproven (**WEAK**).
- The Otago-based booklet tells users that "slight muscle soreness the next day after exercise is normal". Later Life Training booklet: https://westsussex.everyonehealth.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2023/08/LLT-OEP-Booklet-Intermediate-V4a-2018.pdf (**FULL**).

#### Rhabdomyolysis in novices
**WEAK** evidence (narrative reviews and case reports, **ABSTRACT** only):
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34193371/ (Neuromuscul Disord 2021)
- https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5371628 (2017 review)
- https://apcz.umk.pl/JEHS/article/download/69592/45779/226083
- UCLA case reports at journalpub.escholarship.org

What they report:
- Exertional rhabdomyolysis occurs in untrained people as well as athletes and military recruits.
- Unaccustomed exercise, especially eccentric-heavy work, is a recognised trigger. Modifiers include heat, dehydration, NSAIDs, drugs and alcohol, and sickle cell trait.
- Case reports cluster around first spin classes and CrossFit-style high-volume sessions.
- I found no incidence data per session.

Relevant app content: strict pull-up negatives, high-rep pistol squats or split squats, and Copenhagen planks in the first session. Treat novice eccentric volume as the risk to cap.

#### Why RPE and RIR are unreliable in novices, and what to use instead
- **RIR accuracy:** in a scoping review (Halperin et al.) people typically underpredict reps in reserve by about 1 rep. Accuracy is better close to failure and on lower-rep sets. The effect of experience is inconsistent; some studies find none (Remmert 2023; Refalo 2024).
- **Zourdos et al. 2016:** novice and experienced squatters differed in RPE at maximal loads. Source: Stronger by Science summary at https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve (**SECONDARY**).
- **ACSM 2026:** "insufficient evidence to quantify exact RIR and perceived exertion targets" (**FULL**).
- **Honest summary:** the evidence that novices are specifically *worse* at RIR is weaker than folklore suggests. The bigger issue is that novices have never been near failure, so they have no anchor. RIR and RPE are still usable as rough guides.
- **Alternatives used by published programmes:**
  - **Rep-range ceilings** (double progression): ACSM 2009, "1-2 repetitions over the desired number" means increase the load. ROS: use a weight "you can lift ... 8-12 times, up to 3 sets, but not much more. If you find you can easily do more, increase".
  - **Rep-capacity calibration:** Vivifrail adjusts the water bottle until the person can do "about 30 repetitions with some effort", then re-tests after 6 weeks.
  - **Mastery criteria** for balance and skill: hold or steps "eventually build up to 10" (Otago-based booklet); "mastered" (ICFSR).
  - **The talk test** for cardio (section 6).
  - **Observer cues** (breathing, ability to talk) for people with cognitive impairment (ICFSR).

### 4. Safety and pre-participation screening

#### ACSM 2015 pre-participation screening algorithm (Riebe et al., MSSE 2015;47:2473-9)
Algorithm details from Whitfield, Riebe et al., MSSE 2017: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7059860 (**FULL**).

**Inputs**
1. Current activity. "Exerciser" means planned, structured moderate exercise for at least 30 min on at least 3 days/week for the past 3 months.
2. Known cardiovascular, metabolic (type 1 or type 2 diabetes) or renal disease.
3. Signs or symptoms.
4. Desired intensity.

Cardiovascular risk-factor counting was dropped.

**Non-exercisers**
- No disease and no symptoms: start light-to-moderate exercise without clearance and progress gradually.
- Known disease without symptoms: medical clearance before any exercise.
- Symptoms: medical clearance.

**Exercisers**
- No disease and no symptoms: continue moderate or vigorous exercise.
- Known disease without symptoms: continue moderate exercise, but get clearance before vigorous.
- Symptoms: stop and get clearance.

**Signs and symptoms**
- Chest, neck, jaw or arm pain or discomfort, or other discomfort that may be ischaemic
- Shortness of breath at rest or with mild exertion
- Dizziness or syncope
- Orthopnoea or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea
- Ankle oedema
- Palpitations or tachycardia
- Intermittent claudication
- Known heart murmur
- Unusual fatigue or breathlessness with usual activities

**Intensity bands**

| Band | HRR | METs | RPE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | 30 to <40% | 2 to <3 | 9-11 |
| Moderate | 40 to <60% | 3 to <6 | 12-13 |
| Vigorous | ≥60% | ≥6 | ≥14 |

**Referral rates:** applied to NHANES adults aged 40 and over, 54.2% would be referred before any exercise, rising to 70.9% at age 70 and over. That is still the lowest referral rate of the tools compared (PAR-Q 68.4%, AAPQ 94.5%).

#### PAR-Q+ (2025 version, PAR-Q+ Collaboration; form dated 01-11-2024)
URL: https://southlake.ca/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/PARQPlus2025-Form-fillable.pdf (**FULL**). Official home: https://eparmedx.com.

**Seven general questions**
1. Has a doctor said you have a heart condition or high blood pressure?
2. Do you have chest pain at rest, during daily activities, or during physical activity?
3. Do you lose balance because of dizziness, or have you lost consciousness in the last 12 months? (Answer no if the dizziness came from over-breathing.)
4. Do you have another chronic condition?
5. Do you take prescribed medication for a chronic condition?
6. Do you have a bone, joint or soft tissue problem in the past 12 months that activity could worsen?
7. Has a doctor said you should only do medically supervised activity?

**If every answer is no:** "cleared"; "start slowly and build up gradually"; and if you are over 45 and not used to vigorous-to-maximal effort, consult a qualified exercise professional before working at that intensity.

**Any yes** leads to the follow-up pages:
- Arthritis, osteoporosis or back problems
- Cancer
- Heart conditions (including "coronary artery disease and have not participated in regular physical activity")
- High blood pressure (including resting BP of 160/90 or above)
- Metabolic conditions
- Mental health
- Respiratory disease
- Spinal cord injury
- Stroke
- Other

These route to ePARmed-X+.

**Delay becoming more active** if you have a temporary illness, if you are pregnant (talk to a practitioner or complete ePARmed-X+), or if your health changes.

**Licence:** "You must use the entire questionnaire and NO changes are permitted."

> **App implication:** the app cannot embed an edited or partial PAR-Q+. Either link to the official form, ask our own (non-PAR-Q-branded) questions modelled on the ACSM algorithm, or seek permission.

#### Red-flag symptoms: stop exercising
- **Otago-based booklet (FULL):** if you have "chest pain, dizziness or severe shortness of breath, stop immediately and contact your GP (or call an ambulance if you feel very unwell and your symptoms do not go away when you stop exercising)". Joint or muscle pain: "stop, check your position and try again"; if it persists, seek advice.
- **ACSM symptom list above.** Any of these appearing is a "stop and get checked" trigger, not just a pre-screen item.
- **UK NHS-trust and Moving Medicine material (SECONDARY):** dizziness, chest pain or irregular heartbeat means stop and don't restart the session. Palpitations are concerning mainly when combined with chest pain, dizziness, blurred vision or severe breathlessness.
- **WHO:** new symptoms when increasing activity mean consult a healthcare provider.

#### Conditions that need adapted exercise

**Hypertension**
- **PAR-Q+:** resting BP of 160/90 or above, or poorly controlled BP, triggers follow-up.
- **NSCA:** avoid training during acute BP elevation from uncontrolled hypertension; controlled hypertension is generally safe (**SECONDARY**).
- **Vivifrail:** lists uncontrolled BP as a contraindication (**FULL**).
- **ACSM 2019 pronouncement (Pescatello):** https://acsm.org/exercise-hypertension (**FULL** for the text; the FITT table is an image I could not read). 90-150 min/week or more of multimodal moderate exercise; BP falls after a single session and the effect lasts up to 24 h.
- **Isometrics (Edwards et al., BJSM 2023;57:1317):** https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/57/20/1317.full.pdf (**ABSTRACT / SECONDARY**).
  - Network meta-analysis of 270 RCTs, 15,827 participants.
  - Isometric training gave the largest resting BP reduction (-8.24/-4.00 mmHg). The wall squat ranked top for systolic BP.
  - Typical protocol: 4×2 min holds with 2 min rest, 3x/week.
- **Valsalva:** a New Zealand Defence Force handout says avoid breath-holding when lifting, and don't exercise if resting systolic BP is above 200 (diastolic cut-off not visible). Source: https://health.nzdf.mil.nz/assets/Documents/Exercising-with-High-Blood-Pressure-v2.pdf (**SECONDARY**). I did not verify the classic ACSM 200/110 contraindication in a primary source.

**Type 2 diabetes (Kanaley et al., ACSM consensus, MSSE 2022;54:353-68)**
URL: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8802999/ (**FULL**, first 100k characters).
- **Clearance:** low-to-moderate activity such as brisk walking needs no evaluation "unless symptoms of CVD or microvascular complications are present". Sedentary adults should get clearance before moderate-to-high intensity.
- **Hypoglycaemia:** people on insulin or secretagogues (for example sulfonylureas) should supplement carbohydrate or reduce insulin as needed.
- **Retinopathy:** with unstable proliferative or severe retinopathy, avoid "vigorous, high-intensity activities that involve breath holding (e.g., weight lifting and isometrics)" and head-down or jarring activities.
- **Peripheral neuropathy:** limit "prolonged hiking, jogging, or walking on uneven surfaces"; consider cycling or swimming; check feet daily.
- **Autonomic neuropathy:** watch for hypoglycaemia, abnormal BP responses and impaired thermoregulation; use RPE.
- **Resistance training:** 2-3 days/week, "never on consecutive days", 1-3 sets of 10-15 reps.
- **Sitting:** break up sitting with frequent activity breaks.
- **Beta-blockers:** use RPE, not heart rate.

**Osteoporosis**
- **LIFTMOR (Watson et al., JBMR 2018;33:211):** https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709 (**ABSTRACT / SECONDARY**).
  - 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass (T-score below -1).
  - 8 months, twice weekly, 30 min, *supervised*: 5×5 at over 85% 1RM (deadlift, squat, overhead press) plus jumping chin-ups with drop landings.
  - Bone density and function improved.
  - One adverse event (a minor lower back spasm).
  - A 2019 follow-up found no increase in vertebral fractures and improved kyphosis.
  - Caveat: small, selected and highly supervised. This does **not** license unsupervised heavy lifting in an app.
- **UK consensus "Strong, steady and straight" (Brooke-Wavell et al., BJSM 2022;56:837):** https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/15/837 (**ABSTRACT / SECONDARY**).
  - Do resistance and impact exercise, strength and balance work, and spinal extension.
  - Avoid "a high degree of spinal flexion".
  - After vertebral fracture, impact is usually kept to about brisk-walking level.
  - Don't restrict activity on the basis of bone density alone.
- **Royal Osteoporosis Society factsheet "Exercises to promote bone and muscle strength" (2019):** https://strwebstgmedia.blob.core.windows.net/media/z2mhsbs2/exercises-to-promote-bone-and-muscle-strength-fact-sheet-february-2019.pdf (**FULL**).
  - 2-3 non-consecutive days/week, 8-12 reps, up to 3 sets.
  - Progress when you "can easily do more".
  - Jumps 5-10 times, up to 3 sets. People with spinal fractures start with heel drops (toes on the floor) and progress to a small jump only if steady and pain-free.
  - A secondary source says the ROS suggests building to about 50 moderate impacts on most days (**SECONDARY**).
- **Too Fit To Fracture (Giangregorio et al., Osteoporos Int 2014):** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24281053/ (**ABSTRACT**) plus the Osteoporosis Canada handout (**SECONDARY**).
  - Multicomponent resistance and balance training; don't do aerobic training to the exclusion of these.
  - Handout: resistance training 2x/week at 8-12 reps; balance daily for 15-20 min.
  - With a spine fracture, focus on form and alignment rather than effort.

**Osteoarthritis**
- **NICE NG226 (2022):** https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng226 (**SECONDARY** excerpts). Offer tailored therapeutic exercise (local strengthening plus general aerobic). "Advise people with osteoarthritis that joint pain may increase when they start therapeutic exercise." Long-term adherence increases the benefit.
- **bpac NZ (SECONDARY):** discomfort is okay, but if it disturbs sleep or lasts into the next day, reduce intensity or duration.
- **Pain-monitoring model (Thomeé; Silbernagel et al. AJSM 2007, Achilles tendinopathy):** pain up to about 5/10 that settles by next morning and doesn't worsen week to week (**SECONDARY**; sources disagree on "reach" versus "exceed" 5). It comes from tendinopathy, not osteoarthritis, so extrapolation is **WEAK**.
- **ICFSR:** isometrics, then pain-free range, then full range during flares. Low-load fast power work risks meniscus and tendon injury.

**Low back pain:** NICE NG59 (2016, retained after the July 2026 update): "Consider a group exercise programme (biomechanical, aerobic, mind-body or a combination)". No single exercise type is favoured. Source: FHT blog and physitrack summary (**SECONDARY**).

**Pregnancy (note only)**
- WHO: at least 150 min/week moderate activity for women without contraindications, plus daily pelvic floor training.
- Canadian 2019 guideline (Mottola et al., BJSM 2018;52:1339) (**SECONDARY**).
  - Absolute contraindications include ruptured membranes, premature labour, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding and others.
  - Relative contraindications need a discussion with the obstetric provider.
  - Avoid excessive heat, contact or fall-risk activities, and scuba diving.
- PAR-Q+: pregnancy means talk to a practitioner or complete ePARmed-X+.

**After a cardiac event**
- Cardiac rehabilitation referral after MI is an ACC/AHA Class I recommendation. Home-based rehab is an accepted alternative for low-risk patients (AACVPR/AHA/ACC 2019 statement; https://acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/ten-points-to-remember/2019/05/13/10/08/home-based-cardiac-rehabilitation-a-scientific-statement; **SECONDARY**).
- The ACSM algorithm (known CVD and inactive means clearance) and PAR-Q+ Q1/Q3 both route these users out.
- Vivifrail contraindications include recent MI or unstable angina, uncontrolled arrhythmia, severe aortic stenosis, acute heart failure, uncontrolled postural hypotension, and a fracture in the last month (for strength training).

### 5. Regressions by movement pattern, and when to move on

#### Published progression systems found

**Vivifrail (Izquierdo et al.; EU Erasmus+)**
URL: https://cgakit.com/_files/ugd/2a1cfa_1500496832bd44e98195217ce423aaf1.pdf (**FULL**, prescription guide; the passports themselves not read).
- **Assessment:**
  - SPPB from 0 to 12 (balance, 4 m walk, 5 chair rises with arms crossed).
  - If SPPB can't be done, a 6 m walk instead.
  - Fall risk is flagged by any of: 2 or more falls in the past year (or 1 needing medical care); Timed Up and Go over 20 s; 6 m walk over 7.5 s; diagnosed cognitive impairment.
- **Bands:**

| Type | SPPB | 6 m walk | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0-3 | >12 s | Disability; "cannot rise from a chair or a bed"; daily round of about 30-45 min |
| B | 4-6 | 12-7.5 s | Frail; daily 45-60 min |
| C | 7-9 | 7.4-6 s | Pre-frail; daily 45-60 min |
| D | 10-12 | <6 s | Robust; daily 45-60 min |

  The "+" versions (B+, C+) add fall-risk measures. Each programme lasts 12 weeks.
- **Load calibration:** about 30 reps "with some effort" with a water bottle, re-set after 6 weeks.
- **Progression:** re-test at 12 weeks and re-band. I did not see a within-programme progression rule.
- There is an official Vivifrail app.

**Otago Exercise Programme (University of Otago; Campbell)**
- Up to 17 tailored strength and balance exercises plus a walking component; adjustable ankle weights on 3 exercises, progressively increased (NCOA and Texas A&M pages, **SECONDARY**).
- The Later Life Training Otago-based intermediate booklet (**FULL**) shows the progression pattern:
  - Reps or steps "2 times, eventually build up to 10".
  - Heel-toe stand "2 seconds, eventually build up to 10 seconds".
  - Support: "hold support", then "hands near support but not holding on".
  - Exercises at least 2-3x/week plus a daily walk.
- **Licence:** the booklet is © Later Life Training, so do not copy it.

**NHS Strength and Flex** (**SECONDARY**, podcast listings and a forum)
- 5 podcasts over 5 weeks; do each one 3 times a week before moving on.
- By week 5: press-ups and squats.
- The series dates from about 2012, and users report week 5 is demanding for true beginners.

**LIFTMOR:** see section 4. A supervised, heavy, osteopenic-population protocol, not a regression ladder.

**ROS Strong Bones factsheet:** see section 4. Each exercise has modifications (chair, wall, support), 8-12 reps up to 3 sets, and progresses when the reps are easy.

**Too Fit To Fracture / BoneFit:** see section 4.

**Backward chaining (getting down to and up from the floor)**
- Later Life Training method, cited from Skelton's FaME work. Learn to get down to the floor step by step, then reverse the sequence to get up.
- Booklet: "only complete backward chaining in consultation with a therapist or your exercise instructor".
- Evidence: a 2025 pilot RCT in long-term care showed reduced fear of falling and improved life-space mobility (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12347776/; **ABSTRACT**; **WEAK**, pilot).
- A Skelton-led RCT (NCT06823882) is registered; I found no results.

**SilverSneakers:** a commercial programme. I found no published progression criteria and did not research it further.

#### Push
- **Wurm, Ebben et al. (ISBS 2010 abstract; JSCR 2011)** (**ABSTRACT** plus secondary figures):
  - Hands elevated at 61 cm: about 41% of body mass
  - Kneeling push-up: about 49%
  - Hands elevated at 30 cm: about 55%
  - Standard push-up: about 64%
  - Feet elevated: about 70-74%
  - A wall push-up was not measured; it is lower than the 61 cm incline by inference only.
- **Ladder:** wall push-up, then counter or kitchen-worktop incline, then table or bench incline, then knee push-up, then full push-up. Lower the hand height in small steps. Knee push-ups are similar in load to a mid-height incline, so the two are interchangeable steps.
- **Seated options:** ROS-style band chest press, and seated push against a wall or band.
- **When to progress:** use the double-progression rule. When 2-3 sets reach the top of the range (for example 12-15) with good form and are "easy", lower the incline one step (ACSM 2009 / ROS logic). No study validates a specific rep threshold for moving between push-up steps; that part is convention (**WEAK**).

#### Squat and sit-to-stand
- **Biomechanics** (several studies, **SECONDARY / ABSTRACT** via search): higher seats reduce hip and knee moments. Armrests mainly reduce hip moment, with little shown effect on the knee.
- **Ladder:** sit-to-stand from a high chair pushing with the hands, then hands on thighs, then arms crossed, then a lower chair, then box squat (touch and go), then bodyweight squat, then goblet squat. ICFSR's power extension: rise faster, then add "a slight jump at the end".
- **Readiness markers:**
  - **5-times chair stand** (Vivifrail/SPPB; arms crossed): under 11.19 s scores 4/4, 13.70-16.69 s scores 2, over 60 s scores 0.
  - **30 s chair stand**, CDC STEADI: below average for ages 60-64 is under 14 for men and under 12 for women (https://www.nj.gov/humanservices/doas/forms/STEADI-Assessment-30Sec-508.pdf, **SECONDARY** table). Rikli and Jones norms.
  - These are tests, not progression rules. A practical rule is to progress when the current level is done for 2-3 sets of 10-15 with control.
- **Otago-based "knee bends":** 2 reps building to 10, with hands near the support rather than holding it.

#### Hinge
- Glute bridge (ROS "bridge" A3, **FULL**), then hip hinge with hands on a counter, then a light kettlebell deadlift from a raised block.
- With vertebral osteoporosis, avoid loaded spinal flexion (UK consensus). With obesity, the bridge needs floor access; an alternative is a standing hip hinge or a seated "good morning" (**WEAK**, convention).
- I found no published criteria for moving between hinge steps.

#### Pull
- **Ladder:** band row (seated or standing), then table or high-bar inverted row (body near vertical), lowering the bar step by step, then scapular pulls / active hang (if hanging is tolerated), then band-assisted or foot-assisted pull-up, then negatives, then a full pull-up.
- **Inverted-row load** rises with how horizontal the body is. One study reports about 37-79% of body weight across four angles (**SECONDARY**, the angle mapping is unverified). Giancotti et al. JSCR 2021 found load proportional to inclination and strap length (**ABSTRACT**).
- **Negatives** are eccentric-heavy, which is the rhabdomyolysis and muscle-soreness risk pattern. Cap negative volume in the first weeks (section 3; **WEAK** evidence base).
- **Hanging** loads the shoulder. NSCA notes "special care with the shoulder"; ICFSR says screen for rotator cuff tears before power work.
- No published progression criteria found. Use double progression.

#### Lunge and single leg
- Otago-based booklet (**FULL**) has supported exercises: back knee strengthener, sideways walking, heel-toe stand and walk, all with "hold a secure support" safety points.
- **Ladder** (convention, **WEAK**): supported split squat (hand on chair, short range), then full-range split squat, then step-ups onto a low step (ICFSR lists step-ups as functional), then a higher step, then reverse lunge, then Bulgarian split squat.
- Step-ups double as aerobic work in ICFSR Table 1.

#### Core
- ROS factsheet "all fours" exercises (**FULL**).
- Lee 2025: dead bug and bird dog (supine and quadruped), progressing to unstable surfaces only after stable control (**WEAK**).
- **Ladder** (convention): dead bug or bird dog, then wall or incline plank (hands on a counter), then knee plank, then plank. For ab rollouts, require a solid plank first.
- **Osteoporosis:** the UK consensus says avoid high spinal flexion, so no crunches or sit-ups for people with osteoporosis; prefer extension and anti-movement exercises.
- **Seated option:** seated marching, and seated "brace and reach".

#### Carries
- I found no specific guidance for frail or obese populations.
- ICFSR suggests carrying a weighted backpack or waist belt to raise walking intensity, and "holding a heavy object out to one side while maintaining balance" as a balance progression.

#### Balance
- **ICFSR ladder:** feet together holding a chair, then less support, then semi-tandem, tandem, single-leg, eyes closed or foam, perturbation, then dual-task.
- **SPPB thresholds** (Vivifrail):
  - Side-by-side stance: 10 s scores 1.
  - Semi-tandem: 10 s scores 1.
  - Tandem: 10 s scores 2; 3-9 s scores 1; under 3 s scores 0.
- **Otago:** tandem stance "2 seconds, eventually build up to 10 seconds"; heel-toe walking "2 steps ... build up to 10".
- **Progress when mastered:** 10 s holds or 10 steps without touching the support (Otago and SPPB anchors).
- **Dose for falls effect:** a high balance challenge for at least 3 h/week across all exercise (Sherrington).

#### Seated and chair-only options
Chair-based programmes improve handgrip, the 30 s arm curl and the 30 s chair stand (Klempel 2021). Options include:
- Seated band press and row
- Seated knee extension (Vivifrail lists knee extension)
- Bottle curls and press (Vivifrail)
- Ankle movements (Otago)
- Sit-to-stand as the bridge out of chair-only work
- Recumbent cycling, seated steppers or arm ergometry for cardio (ICFSR)

### 6. Cardio for deconditioned people

- **Starting dose:**
  - ICFSR: 5-10 min (or less), progressing to 15-30 min, 3-7 days/week, splittable into short bouts. Prefer walking variants; if balance or arthritis is poor, use recumbent cycling or water.
  - EASO/ESSA weight-related doses are targets to work towards, not starting points.
- **Steps (Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health 2022;7:e219):** https://doaj.org/article/c3639c468b4348958202e52793ab979d (**ABSTRACT**).
  - Mortality benefit plateaus at about 6,000-8,000 steps/day for people aged 60 and over, and about 8,000-10,000 for under-60s.
  - Relevance: 10,000 is not a necessary goal for older users. Progress from the person's baseline.
- **NHS Couch to 5K** (**SECONDARY**): 9 weeks, 3 runs/week with rest days. Week 1 is a 5 min walk, then 60 s run / 90 s walk alternating for 20 min. It is a *running* programme, so it is a later step, not level 0 for older, obese or very deconditioned users.
- **Japanese interval walking (Nemoto et al., Mayo Clin Proc 2007;82:803):** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17605959/ (**ABSTRACT**).
  - 246 adults, mean age 63.
  - Protocol: 5 or more sets of 3 min fast walking (at least 70% of peak aerobic capacity) alternating with 3 min slow walking (40%), 4 or more days/week, for 5 months.
  - Results versus continuous moderate walking: knee extension strength +13%, flexion +17%, peak aerobic capacity +8-9%, and a larger systolic BP fall.
  - A good middle rung between plain walking and HIIT. Single-centre trial.
- **Talk test:**
  - Reed and Pipe, Curr Opin Cardiol 2014 (**ABSTRACT**): "valid, reliable, practical, and inexpensive" for healthy adults and people with cardiovascular disease.
  - Dehart-Beverley 2000 (**ABSTRACT**): comfortable talking means at or below the ventilatory threshold; clearly failing it means above.
  - Vieira 2022 systematic review in cardiopulmonary patients (**ABSTRACT**): correlates with the first ventilatory threshold, but reliability evidence is thin.
  - Practical anchors: zone 2 / moderate is "can talk in full sentences"; vigorous is "a few words only".
- **Heart rate zones become invalid with** beta-blockers, atrial fibrillation or a pacemaker. ICFSR and Kanaley 2022 both say use RPE or perceived exertion instead. Phentermine also alters heart rate (ESSA). The app's heart-rate-based zone 2 needs a "my heart rate isn't reliable" switch that falls back to the talk test or RPE.

### Implications for an app

Each rule cites the source behind it. Wording stays within "suggests, the person decides"; none of this is diagnosis.

#### A. Screening (onboarding, and re-asked when health changes)
1. **Ask our own short questions modelled on the ACSM 2015 algorithm** (current activity, known heart/metabolic/kidney disease, symptoms). Don't embed an edited PAR-Q+: its licence forbids changes, but we can link to the official PAR-Q+ / ePARmed-X+ (PAR-Q+ 2025). Questions:
   - Have you done planned moderate exercise for 30 min or more, 3 or more days/week, for the past 3 months?
   - Has a doctor told you that you have heart disease, diabetes or kidney disease?
   - Do you get chest, neck, jaw or arm discomfort; breathlessness at rest or on mild effort; dizziness or fainting; palpitations; ankle swelling; calf pain when walking; or unusual fatigue? (ACSM symptom list, Whitfield 2017)
   - Has a doctor said you should only exercise under medical supervision? (PAR-Q+ Q7)
   - Have you had a heart attack, heart surgery or a stent recently? (Vivifrail contraindications; ACC/AHA cardiac rehab)
   - Have you had a fracture in the last month? (Vivifrail)
   - Are you pregnant? (PAR-Q+, WHO, Mottola)
   - Do you have osteoporosis or a spine fracture; joint pain or arthritis; high blood pressure (and is it controlled); insulin or sulfonylurea use; diabetic eye or foot problems; or take beta-blockers? (ICFSR, Kanaley, UK osteoporosis consensus)
   - Have you fallen twice or more in the past year, or once needing medical care? (Vivifrail fall-risk test)
2. **"See a doctor first" message** (ACSM 2015): show it when the person has symptoms, or is inactive with known cardiac, metabolic or renal disease. The person can still choose to continue with "light only" content.
3. **Active with known disease but no symptoms:** allow moderate work, but put a "check with your doctor first" interstitial before vigorous content (HIIT, heavy loads) (ACSM 2015).
4. **Healthy and inactive, no symptoms:** no clearance needed. Start light-to-moderate and progress gradually (ACSM 2015; WHO 2020: clearance "generally unnecessary").
5. **Age over 45 and not used to vigorous effort:** before the first HIIT or near-maximal session, suggest consulting an exercise professional (PAR-Q+ cleared-path text).
6. **Re-screen** if the person reports new symptoms or a health change, and at least every 12 months (PAR-Q+ clearance is "valid for a maximum of 12 months").

#### B. Start levels (a "level 0" below today's ladders)
7. **Add a functional baseline test instead of guessing.** Use the 5-times or 30 s chair stand, a tandem-stance hold, wall or incline push-up reps, and an optional 4 m walk. Map results onto Vivifrail-like bands A-D (Vivifrail; SPPB thresholds; CDC STEADI 30 s chair-stand norms).
   - **Band A** (cannot rise from a chair without help): chair-only strength plus supported sit-to-stand. No walking or cardio programme until sit-to-stand improves (Vivifrail Type A; ICFSR sequencing).
   - **Bands B/C:** strength first, then balance, then aerobic work, introduced one modality at a time (ICFSR).
8. **First 1-2 weeks for any untrained user:**
   - 3-4 exercises, 1-2 sets, effort well short of failure ("could do several more"). Below capacity on purpose (ICFSR 30-40% 1RM, 1-2 sets, 3-4 exercises; NSCA frail 20-30% 1RM).
   - Cardio 5-10 min (ICFSR).
   - The aim is the repeated-bout protection effect (Chen/Nosaka) and avoiding an injury that ends adherence (ICFSR).
9. **Hide or lock these at first** for untrained, older or obese users:
   - HIIT 20 s/10 s (EASO: HIIT only after cardiovascular risk assessment and with supervision; Ekkekakis affect evidence)
   - Pistol squats, Bulgarian split squats, strict pull-up negatives, Copenhagen planks, ab rollouts, kettlebell swings
   Unlock each through its regression ladder. Rationale: a sudden eccentric-heavy volume jump is the rhabdomyolysis and muscle-soreness pattern (narrative reviews, **WEAK**). Power and fast movements also carry joint risk where there are undiagnosed degenerative changes (ICFSR).
10. **Long gaps:** after 3 or more weeks off, suggest restarting a level or two lower. Repeated-bout protection is gone by about 3 weeks (Chen/Nosaka; **WEAK**, small studies).

#### C. Progression criteria (what the app checks before suggesting the next rung)
11. **Strength (double progression):** suggest the next harder variation or load when the user completes the top of the rep range (for example 12-15 for beginners) on all prescribed sets with good form and reports it as "easy" or "could do 2+ more", on 2 consecutive sessions (ACSM 2009: "1-2 repetitions over the desired number"; ROS: "if you find you can easily do more, increase"). Load steps 2-10% (ACSM 2009).
12. **Balance and skill:** progress when a 10 s hold or 10 steps is done without touching the support (Otago-based "build up to 10"; SPPB 10 s anchors; ICFSR "mastered"). Support sequence: holding, then hands near, then no hands, then eyes closed or foam, then dual-task (ICFSR; Otago-based).
13. **Volume before intensity in the early weeks:** sets 1 → 2 → 3 (ICFSR; NSCA; ROS "up to three sets").
14. **Cardio:** time first (5-10 → 15-30 min) (ICFSR). Then intervals at walking pace (Japanese interval walking, 3 min fast / 3 min slow). Only then running intervals (Couch to 5K) or HIIT. Use the talk test to gate each step (Reed and Pipe; Dehart-Beverley).
15. **Effort scales for novices:** pair any RIR or RPE prompt with a concrete anchor ("could you do 2-3 more with good form?") and use rep-capacity tests (Vivifrail's about-30-reps calibration). ACSM 2026 says exact RIR targets are not evidence-based, and novices tend to misjudge RIR by about 1 rep (Halperin, **SECONDARY**). Don't use failure: ACSM 2026 says it is unnecessary and possibly inadvisable for older people.
16. **Periodic re-test** (for example every 6-12 weeks) to re-band. Vivifrail re-calibrates load at 6 weeks and runs 12-week blocks.

#### D. Adaptations by group
17. **Older adults:**
    - Multicomponent work 3 or more days/week, with balance in every session (WHO 2020).
    - For a falls effect, total balance-challenging exercise of at least 3 h/week, ongoing (Sherrington 2017/2019).
    - Include power work (fast rise, slow lower, never to failure) once sit-to-stand is solid (ICFSR; NSCA 40-60% 1RM).
18. **Obesity:**
    - Default cardio is low-impact: walking, cycling, water, seated (ESSA Table 3; Messier knee-load data).
    - No jumping early (practitioner advice, **WEAK**).
    - Offer a "no floor work" switch that swaps floor exercises for bench, incline, standing or seated versions. Offer a "no lying flat" option (inclined bench) for people with reflux or breathlessness when lying down. These are comfort adaptations, **WEAK** evidence.
    - Track health and fitness outcomes, not only weight (EASO; Obesity Canada; ESSA).
    - Heat warning on hot days (BMI is associated with heat illness in military data; **WEAK** extrapolation).
    - With GLP-1 drugs, nudge towards resistance training (**SECONDARY**).
19. **Hypertension:**
    - Exhale on effort and don't hold your breath (NZDF handout, **SECONDARY**).
    - If the user says BP is uncontrolled or 160/90 or above, suggest checking with a clinician first (PAR-Q+).
    - Isometric wall squat or handgrip is an option for BP (Edwards 2023), but not for people with severe or proliferative diabetic retinopathy (Kanaley 2022).
20. **Type 2 diabetes:**
    - Resistance training on non-consecutive days (Kanaley).
    - On insulin or sulfonylureas, show a hypoglycaemia reminder before sessions (Kanaley).
    - With neuropathy, prefer cycling or swimming and prompt daily foot checks (Kanaley).
    - With retinopathy, hide heavy lifting, isometrics, head-down and jarring moves (Kanaley).
21. **Osteoporosis:**
    - Hide loaded spinal flexion (crunches, sit-ups, toe-touches) (UK consensus 2022).
    - Prefer extension, resistance work 2-3 days/week at 8-12 reps, and balance work (ROS; Too Fit To Fracture).
    - With spine fractures, impact starts at heel drops; small jumps come only if steady and pain-free (ROS factsheet).
    - Don't present LIFTMOR-style heavy lifting as safe unsupervised (LIFTMOR was fully supervised).
22. **Osteoarthritis:**
    - Warn that joint pain may rise at first (NICE NG226).
    - Suggested pain rule: discomfort during exercise is OK if it settles by next day and doesn't build week to week; otherwise drop a level (bpac; Thomeé/Silbernagel model, **WEAK** extrapolation).
    - During flares, offer isometric versions (ICFSR).
23. **Beta-blockers, atrial fibrillation or pacemaker:** turn off heart rate zones and use the talk test or RPE (ICFSR; Kanaley).
24. **Pregnancy:** don't programme; point to ePARmed-X+ or a clinician (PAR-Q+).
25. **After a cardiac event:** point to cardiac rehab (ACC/AHA Class I), and offer only light content until the user confirms clearance (ACSM 2015).

#### E. Stop signs (in-session "stop" button, and copy shown before every session)
26. **Stop now and seek help** (call emergency services if it doesn't settle with rest) for any of: chest, neck, jaw or arm pain or pressure; dizziness or fainting; severe or unusual breathlessness; palpitations together with any of these. Don't resume that day (Otago-based booklet; ACSM symptom list; NHS trust leaflet, **SECONDARY**).
27. **Sharp joint or muscle pain:** stop, check position, retry; if it persists, skip the exercise and suggest a regression or professional advice (Otago-based booklet).
28. **Normal versus not:** mild next-day muscle soreness is normal (Otago-based booklet). Very severe soreness with swelling or dark (cola-coloured) urine after a hard session means seek medical care (rhabdomyolysis reviews, **WEAK** / standard clinical teaching; wording to be checked with a clinician).
29. **New symptoms between sessions:** pause progression and suggest seeing a clinician (WHO 2020: "Those who develop new symptoms ... should consult a healthcare provider").
30. **Floor recovery:** for older users, teach getting down to and up from the floor via backward chaining as an optional skill, with advice to learn it with an instructor (LLT booklet; pilot RCT, **WEAK**).

#### Biggest gaps and weak spots
- **Rep thresholds for moving between bodyweight variations** (wall → incline → knee → full push-up, row angles, hinge steps): no study validates the exact numbers. This is expert convention plus ACSM's double-progression rule.
- **Obesity-specific positions** (prone, supine with reflux, floor transfers, chafing): only practitioner advice was found; there is no guideline text.
- **Rhabdomyolysis risk per session for novices:** only case reports and narrative reviews.
- **Novice RIR inaccuracy:** the evidence is mixed. The strongest claim supported is that exact RIR or RPE targets are not evidence-based (ACSM 2026).
- **Not read in full:** NSCA 2019 (summary only); the 2023 ACSM heat consensus; the World Falls Guidelines dosing; Obesity Canada recommendation grades; the Otago original manual (only an Otago-based licensed booklet).

### 2. Self-report screening when the person cannot or will not test

- **A global "rate your fitness" item does not place individuals.** Jensen et al. 2018, Int J Sports Med 39(6):419–25, n=749 adults aged 30–60, compared excellent/very good/good/fair/poor against measured cardiorespiratory fitness. Agreement was κ=0.18 (men) and 0.27 (women), γ about 0.63–0.67. Authors: useful for populations, "**not valid for individual assessments**" (https://research.regionh.dk/da/publications/the-association-between-self-rated-fitness-and-cardiorespiratory-/ [abstract]).
- **Subjective judgment of capacity misses low fitness.** In the METS study (Wijeysundera et al., Lancet 2018;391:2631–40), subjective assessment detected inability to reach 4 METs with **19.2% sensitivity** and 94.7% specificity. DASI was the only measure that predicted outcome [abstract/secondary]. In the study, clinicians did the subjective assessment, not the patients themselves. The direction still holds: vague impressions overrate capacity.
- **Duke Activity Status Index (DASI).** 12 yes/no activity items, e.g. walk a block, climb a flight of stairs, run a short distance, heavy housework. Spearman r with peak VO2 was 0.80 in the development sample and **0.58 in the independent validation** (n=50 each; Hlatky et al., Am J Cardiol 1989;64:651–4, https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/734270 [abstract]). Estimated VO2 = 0.43×DASI + 9.6 [secondary]. Concrete "can you do this specific thing?" items work better than global ratings. That is the same pattern as the app's ladder questions.
- **"Can you climb two flights of stairs?"** Some signal, poorly specific: sensitivity about 0.71, specificity about 0.47 for events (Reilly 1999, via secondary). One systematic review found it often did not improve risk models [secondary search summary].
- **SF-36 physical functioning (10 items).** Each item is rated "limited a lot / a little / not at all": vigorous activities, moderate activities, carrying groceries, several flights of stairs, one flight, bending or kneeling, walking more than a mile, several blocks, one block, bathing or dressing. It is a good **floor-end** screen for frail people. RAND holds the copyright; check the RAND-36 terms before shipping [secondary].
- **IPAQ-short** measures activity, not fitness, and is weak. Correlations with objective measures were 0.09–0.39, and it **overestimates activity by about 84% on average** (Lee et al., IJBNPA 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22018588/ [abstract]). Do not use it to place people.
- **Godin leisure-time questionnaire.** Leisure Score Index ≥24 = "active". Supported for classifying healthy adults (Amireault & Godin 2015, n=100). In cancer survivors, agreement with an accelerometer was only 70.8% (sensitivity 75%, specificity 59%) [secondary]. It also measures activity, not capacity.
- **IFIS (International Fitness Scale).** Five 1–5 items: overall, cardiorespiratory, muscular, speed-agility and flexibility fitness. Validated in Spanish young adults (Ortega 2013), in older adults (Merellano-Navarro 2017, results not retrieved) and in adults with type 2 diabetes. In T2D the muscular item was the one that did *not* track outcomes [abstract]. A 2020 reliability review calls for better studies.
- **Review of older-adult self-perceived fitness questionnaires** (2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34216744/ [abstract]): 7 instruments. SRFit had the best concurrent validity and reliability. Authors call for more rigorous instruments.
- **Self-report vs measured, broader picture.** In Norwegian conscripts (n=14,166), r ranged from 0.29 to 0.82; pull-ups were the most accurate item. Concrete counts beat abstract ratings [secondary].
- **Safety screen.** PAR-Q+ (2024/2025 form) is the standard self-administered pre-activity screen [secondary]. A 2024 public-perception study (n=1,019) said it "would need extensive simplification" for lay use [abstract]. Licensing terms were not confirmed.

**Summary for Q2.** Global self-ratings misplace individuals; agreement is only slight to fair. Specific "can you do X?" items do better (DASI ρ≈0.58). Self-reported counts for a concrete movement do best (pull-ups r up to 0.82). Whatever the method, expect some error, so plan to correct it after the first session.

**Patterns:**
1. Almost every app asks goal, experience or level, days, and equipment. Apple, Caliber and Fitbod ask minutes per session; Boostcamp does not.
2. Time is fitted mainly by **cutting the number of exercises** (Fitbod), not by switching to supersets or circuits. I found no app documentation describing superset compression.
3. Only Bold (older adults) uses a physical check at onboarding.

**Things apps do that the evidence does not support:**
- **Level set by training years (Boostcamp) or weekly frequency (old NTC).** Neither measures current capacity. A detrained returner gets placed too high, which is exactly the Freeletics failure. This is my inference; I have no direct study.
- **A global self-rated level as the only placement.** It is not valid for individuals (Jensen 2018) and tends to over-rate low fitness (METS).
- **Large fall-reduction claims (Bold)** that are not independently replicated.
- **Asking beginners for RPE or "too hard" feedback without anchoring it.** Novices underpredict reps in reserve by about 4–5 reps; experienced lifters by about 1–2 (Steele 2017 via review [secondary]). Accuracy improves with practice [abstract, Brazilian study].

### 4. Ladder design

**Published systems:**
- **r/bodyweightfitness Recommended Routine** (https://redditbwf.github.io/wiki/recommended_routine.html [full page, undated mirror; content from the 2018 update]):
  - "Perform the hardest exercise in each progression that you can do for 3 sets of 5 to 8 repetitions."
  - Move on at **3×8 with good form**, then restart the next step at 3×5. For static holds, move on at **3×30 s**.
  - 3×/week, at least one rest day between sessions; strength work takes 40–60 min.
  - **No rule for moving down.**
  - The wiki explains rep ranges as bridging "a significant difference in intensity" between steps.
  - Some third-party copies use 3×8–12 for core work.
- **Convict Conditioning (Wade)** [secondary]:
  - **10 steps per movement**; push-ups run from wall push-up to one-arm push-up.
  - Each step has beginner, intermediate and "progression" standards, but I could not verify the per-step numbers. One unverified claim is 2×50 for early steps; another says 6–20 for steps above 5.
  - Train 2–4 days/week, 30–60 min, and everyone starts at step 1. That start is very conservative.
- **Overcoming Gravity (Low, 2nd ed. 2016).** Level charts (1–20ish) per skill. I could not retrieve the pass criteria online; they are in the book.
- **GMB.** Progresses on movement quality rather than fixed rep gates; no explicit numeric rule found [secondary].
- **Evidence that a calisthenic ladder works.** Kotarsky et al. 2018 (J Strength Cond Res 32(3):651–9, n=23 moderately trained men, 4 weeks): progressive push-up training gave **bench 1RM gains similar to bench-press training** (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466268/ [abstract]). The study was small and short.

**Step size (push-up load as % body mass):**
- **Ebben/Wurm et al. (ISBS 2010; JSCR 2011 25(10):2891–4) [full text of the ISBS paper].** n=23 recreationally fit young adults, about 80 kg; peak force-plate GRF:
  - Hands raised 61 cm: **41%**
  - **Knee (bent): 49%**
  - Hands raised 30 cm: **55%**
  - Standard: **64%**
  - Feet raised 30 cm: **70%**
  - Feet raised 61 cm: **74%**
  - All six differ significantly from each other.
  - Adjacent steps are **4–9 percentage points of body mass apart, about 6–20% relative**.
  - The knee push-up sits **between** the two incline heights.
  - Feet-elevated steps are small jumps (64 → 70 → 74), which is why deficit and archer variants are needed to keep climbing.
- **Gouvali & Boudolos 2005** (cited inside Ebben): standard 66%, knee 53%; hand-width changes span 52.9–72.9%.
- **Suprak et al. 2011** (JSCR 25(2):497–503, n=28 strength-trained men, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20179649/ [abstract]): load is **higher at the bottom** than at the top. Standard push-up about 69% (top) to 75% (bottom); knee push-up about 54% to 62% [numbers secondary, via Cooper Institute blog].
- **Incline by angle.** A Michigan State dissertation (K. Yang) reports a near-linear relation between body angle and hand force at 15/30/45°. Values were not retrieved because the site was unreachable. No wall push-up force-plate value was found.
- **Dhahbi et al. 2022** (Sports Biomech 21(1):1–40) systematically reviews 46 push-up variants. It is paywalled; values not retrieved.

**Summary for Q4.**
- **Number of steps:** typically 6–10 per movement (BWF uses about 6–10 per slot; Convict Conditioning uses 10).
- **Moving up:** a rep target with good form, commonly 3×8 (or 3×30 s for holds), then reset to the low end on the next step.
- **Moving down:** there is **no published rule** in these systems. The app has to define one.
- **Step size:** published load data supports steps of roughly 5–10% of body mass. Rep-range "bridging" covers the gap.

### 5. Time budget UX and an honest minimum

- **WHO 2020 guidelines:**
  - Adults: 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity per week, **plus muscle-strengthening of major muscle groups on ≥2 days per week**.
  - Adults 65+: additionally, **multicomponent balance and strength activity on ≥3 days per week** for fall prevention, now recommended for all older adults rather than only those with poor mobility (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7719906/ [secondary summaries]).
- **Minimum effective strength dose** (Iversen et al., Sports Med 2021, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449772 [full text read to about 60%]):
  - **≥4 weekly sets per muscle group**, 6–15 RM, or 15–40 reps if taken to failure. This is directly useful for bodyweight work.
  - Three multi-joint exercises: a leg press, an upper pull and an upper push.
  - Weekly volume matters more than frequency; one or several short sessions both work.
  - Untrained people: rest 1–2 min.
  - Supersets, drop sets and rest-pause **roughly halve session time** at matched volume, but are "probably better" for hypertrophy than strength. The authors advise against them for heavy compound free-weight lifts.
  - Warm-ups should be exercise-specific only.
- **Fyfe, Hamilton & Daly** (Sports Med 2022;52:463–79 [abstract]): minimal-dose resistance training can preserve strength and function and may improve enjoyment and adherence. A feasibility study ran 5-min, equipment-free home sessions 1–3×/day in people aged 65–79, with effectiveness still being tested [secondary].
- **Lack of time** is consistently among the top reported barriers, but not always first. Motivation, pain and fear rank alongside it, and older adults cite health and fear more often [secondary review summaries].
- **Planning when and where (implementation intentions)** raises activity a little: d=0.31 after the intervention and 0.24 at follow-up across 26 studies (Bélanger-Gravel et al., Health Psychol Rev 2013 [abstract]). This supports asking **which days**, not only how many.
- **What apps ask:** Apple asks days plus minutes per day; Peloton asks days plus duration; Fitbod asks minutes per session; Boostcamp asks days only.

I found **no study comparing ways of asking about time** (per session vs per week vs total minutes). Any choice between them is a product judgment.

**An honest minimum.** The WHO strength component can be met with **2 sessions/week**, each covering legs, push and pull at ≥2 sets. Iversen's ≥4 sets/week per muscle group then fits in roughly **15–25 min per session**. That estimate is mine: about 3 exercises × 2 sets × (about 40 s work + 60–90 s rest), plus a specific warm-up.

That minimum does **not** cover the 150 min of aerobic activity. The app should say so plainly rather than imply a short strength plan meets "the guidelines". For people aged 65+, it should add balance work on ≥3 days. Short balance blocks can be folded into strength sessions.

### Implications for an app

#### A. Onboarding questions, in order

1. **Safety screen.** Use PAR-Q+-style yes/no questions: heart condition, chest pain at rest or on exertion, dizziness or fainting, bone or joint problem worsened by activity, medication for blood pressure or heart, pregnancy. Any "yes" leads to "talk to a clinician first", and the app offers only the gentlest ladders.
   - *Sources:* PAR-Q+ [secondary]; ATS 6MWT contraindications; Mittleman 1993 on exertion risk.
   - Simplify the wording: the 2024 perception study found the form hard for lay users.
2. **Age band, and pain or injury areas** (Boostcamp-style body-part list). Age decides whether balance work is suggested (WHO 65+). Injuries filter exercises out.
3. **A floor-end function check, worded like the SF-36 physical functioning items or DASI** ("Does your health limit you in climbing one flight of stairs / walking one block / getting up from a chair without your hands?").
   - *Sources:* SF-36 PF; DASI (Hlatky 1989).
   - Any limitation routes the person to the lowest rungs and to balance ladders. This catches frail people that a global self-rating misses (METS: 19% sensitivity for subjective assessment).
4. **Placement per movement** using concrete "Can you do…?" ladder questions (see B). Do **not** ask "rate your fitness" or "years of training" as the placement input.
   - *Sources:* Jensen 2018 (not valid for individuals); my inference from Boostcamp and Freeletics.
   - Optionally ask "trained before?" only to tune the rep target.
5. **Equipment** (none / chair / table / bar / bands / weights).
6. **Which days** of the week (tap days, as in Apple Fitness+), which gives frequency.
   - *Source:* implementation intentions, Bélanger-Gravel 2013.
7. **Minutes per session**, with the honest-minimum note: "Under about 15 min we will cover fewer movements; 2 days a week is the WHO strength minimum; walking or cycling for 150 min/week is separate."
   - *Sources:* WHO 2020; Iversen 2021.
8. **Goal** (stay independent / get stronger / build muscle / general fitness), used for exercise emphasis only. It is asked last because it changes the least.

#### B. Placement method: questions first, an optional quick test, corrected by the first session

- **Default: specific capability questions per ladder step.** For example, "Can you do 8 push-ups from the knees with a straight back?", where a yes moves up and a no stops.
  - This is DASI-like: concrete activity items beat global ratings (DASI ρ≈0.58 vs single-item κ 0.18–0.27).
  - Make each question name a **rep count and a form cue**, matching the ladder's move-up criterion. Self-reported concrete counts were the most accurate in the conscript data.
  - When unsure, place the person **one step below** the last "yes". The cost of starting too low is a session or two; the cost of starting too high is injury and drop-out (Freeletics case). This rule is my own design judgment.
- **Optional "try it now" checks**, offered rather than required, using only tests that are safe alone:
  - 30-s chair stand, compared with STEADI/Rikli norms for 60+.
  - Push-up attempt from wall → incline → knee → floor.
  - Balance stances next to a counter.
- **Avoid as onboarding tests:**
  - 12-min run and other maximal efforts.
  - 6MWT, TUG and floor sit-rise for frail or obese people.
  - Plank, wall sit or dead hang "norms", because none are credible.
- **The first logged session is the real placement.** If logged reps fall short of the step's minimum, suggest stepping down. Stepping down **must** work, unlike the Freeletics case.
- **The person decides.** Present placement as "we suggest starting at X; tap to choose another".

#### C. Ladder-step rules

- **Order steps by measured load where data exist.**
  - Push-up order by Ebben: hands-high incline 41% → **knee 49%** → low incline 55% → floor 64% → feet 30 cm 70% → feet 61 cm 74%, then deficit and archer.
  - The knee push-up belongs between the two incline heights, not directly below the floor push-up.
  - Add a wall push-up below the highest incline for the frail end. Convict Conditioning starts at wall push-ups; no force data was found for the wall push-up.
- **Step size target:** about 5–10% of body mass between adjacent steps, per Ebben's spacing. Where no load data exist, use rep-range bridging.
- **Move up:** **3 sets of 8 with good form**, then start the next step at 3×5 and add reps each session. For holds, 3×30 s.
  - *Source:* BWF Recommended Routine.
  - For very deconditioned people, a lower entry range (e.g. 2×5) is defensible under minimal-dose evidence (Fyfe 2022). This is an inference.
- **Move down:** this needs a rule the app invents, because no source gives one. Suggested: if the person cannot reach the bottom of the range (e.g. fewer than 5 reps with good form) on two consecutive sessions, or reports pain, suggest the previous step.
  - Rationale: the Freeletics failure case.
- **Use rep counts plus a yes/no form check, not RPE or RIR, for beginners.** Novices underpredict reps in reserve by about 4–5 (Steele 2017 via review [secondary]). Introduce RIR later, once the person has some practice.
- **Volume floor per session:** cover legs, push and pull. Aim for ≥4 weekly sets per pattern across the week (Iversen 2021), on ≥2 days (WHO).
- **Fitting a short time budget:**
  1. First drop accessory or extra exercises (as Fitbod does).
  2. Then drop to 2 sets.
  3. Then pair non-competing moves (e.g. push + squat) as supersets to roughly halve rest time (Iversen 2021).
  - Keep the three main patterns as the last thing cut.
  - For 65+, keep a short balance block.
- **Steps per ladder:** about 6–10, matching BWF and Convict Conditioning. Fewer steps means bigger load jumps.

#### Evidence gaps to flag in the proposal

- No validation of fully self-scored, unsupervised home fitness tests.
- No published "move down" rule.
- No load data for wall push-ups or for non-push-up ladders (squat, row, hinge) in this pass.
- No UX research on how to phrase time questions.
- Rikli & Jones 1999/2013 tables, Araújo SRT norms and CSEP push-up tables were not retrieved in primary form.
- App behaviour comes from help pages and reviews, not hands-on inspection.

## Part 4: Europe: safety questions, the law, and keeping the app in Europe

Researched 31 Oct 2026 for ADR-0085. Not legal or medical advice: the legal parts are a briefing
for a lawyer, the medical parts for a clinician.

How to read the marks:

- **[full]**: I read the whole primary text, or the whole relevant section of it.
- **[part]**: I read the primary text, but only the sections named.
- **[abstract]**: abstract or official summary only.
- **[secondary]**: a law firm, review or search summary describing the source; not checked
  against the original.
- **(derived)**: my inference, not something a source says.


### Recommendation

#### 1. Safety questions: what Europe expects

1. **There is no single European pre-exercise form.** Europe's bodies do not prescribe one.
   - The ESC 2020 guideline asks for "a self-assessment relating to symptoms" plus a
     cardiovascular risk score, then medical checks only for sedentary or high-risk people
     who plan high-intensity exercise.
   - Germany's DGSP 2024 guideline is a doctor's examination before intense sport, not a
     questionnaire. It has a recorded dissent from the GPs' society (DEGAM): offering exams
     widely could put people off moving.
   - The UK consensus (Reid 2022, with FSEM and Sport England) and WHO 2020 say routine medical
     clearance is unnecessary if people build up gradually. People should seek advice when they
     have symptoms.
   - PAR-Q (old version) is common in gyms in the UK and Germany. PAR-Q+ is the international
     research standard, but no European body adopts it as its standard (not confirmed for
     EuropeActive; see Gaps).
2. **Keep our own questions; don't embed PAR-Q+.** Its terms need prior written permission to put
   it in an app or website, even unedited, and they allow fees. Linking to the official page and
   to the official German PDF on eparmedx.com is fine (derived: a link copies nothing).
   Translations exist in German, Spanish and Turkish (plus North-American French). Dutch,
   Finnish, Greek, Norwegian and Slovenian are in progress. There is no Italian, Polish or
   European Portuguese version.
3. **Ask 10 yes-or-no questions, in our own words** (the table in Evidence 1.9 traces each one):
   1. In the last 3 months, have you done planned exercise that made you breathe harder, on at
      least 3 days in most weeks?
   2. Are you 35 or older?
   3. Has a doctor told you that you have a heart condition, or have you had a heart attack, a
      stent, heart surgery or a stroke?
   4. Do you have diabetes or kidney disease?
   5. Has a doctor told you that your blood pressure is high and not yet under control?
   6. In the last month, have you had pain, pressure or tightness in your chest, neck, jaw or
      arms, at rest or when active?
   7. Do you get unusually out of breath, or feel your heart racing or beating irregularly, with
      light effort such as walking or one flight of stairs?
   8. In the last 12 months, have you fainted, or fallen because you felt dizzy?
   9. Has a doctor told you to exercise only under supervision, or not to exercise for now?
   10. Are you pregnant, or have you had a baby in the last 6 months?

   "Not sure" counts as yes (derived).
4. **Show one of three notes.** The person can always continue, and no note blocks anything
   (ADR-0046):
   - **A. "Talk to a doctor before you start."** For a yes to 6, 7, 8 or 9. For 10, the note
     says "your midwife or doctor".
   - **B. "Start easy, and ask a doctor before hard sessions."** For a yes to 3, 4 or 5 with no
     symptoms, or for no to 1 and yes to 2.
   - **C. "Start easy and build up."** Everyone else.

   Every note also shows the stop signs, says "if your health changes, answer again", and
   offers the questions again after 12 months.
5. **Words to avoid:** "cleared", "safe for you", "risk score", "screening result" and
   "medical clearance". Use "we suggest" and "you decide". Don't show a SCORE2 or other risk
   calculation (see 2a).

#### 2. Law

**(a) Medical device (MDR 2017/745, MDCG 2019-11 rev.1, June 2025).**

- MDCG 2019-11 says plainly that "wellness or fitness apps, do not qualify as MDSW". What
  decides it is the **intended purpose the maker states**, in the app, the documentation and
  the marketing.
- The line is crossed by software that acts on one person's data for "diagnosis, prevention,
  monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease". Examples:
  - a heart-risk score;
  - "you're cleared" or "you're at risk";
  - analysing heart rate to warn of illness (MDCG names "preventing the risk of illnesses by
    analysing physiological parameters" as class IIa);
  - exercise plans adapted to a declared disease (derived: that is treatment or alleviation).
- To stay a fitness app:
  1. Publish an intended-purpose statement (draft in Evidence 2.1).
  2. Give the same three generic notes to anyone, drawn from public guidance. They point to a
     doctor and never judge the person.
  3. Keep "keep me off the floor" and similar as preferences without reasons.
  4. Turn Part D of the research doc (adaptations by disease: diabetes, retinopathy,
     osteoporosis and the like) into neutral preferences, or drop it (derived).
  5. Label the heart-rate switch "Use the talk test instead of heart-rate zones", not "I take
     beta-blockers".
  6. Make no disease claims in marketing, for example "lowers blood pressure" or "for
     arthritis" (derived; the German HWG may also apply to such claims).

**(b) GDPR Art. 9.**

- **Answers checked only in the browser, never sent and not kept**, is the strongest design:
  - nothing reaches the Pi, Cloudflare (which decrypts traffic), the logs or the backups;
  - there is nothing to breach, export or erase.
- It does not settle **for certain** that the UG isn't a controller. The CJEU holds that a
  controller need not have access to the data (Wirtschaftsakademie C-210/16, Jehovan todistajat
  C-25/17), and the UG writes the questions and the logic. Some industry bodies argue that no
  controller processes purely local data. This is unsettled, so the lawyer should decide.
  Until then, say in the privacy notice that the answers are checked on the device and never
  sent (derived).
- **Sending but not storing is still processing** (Art. 4(2) lists collection, use and
  transmission). It would need explicit consent under Art. 9(2)(a), and the answers would pass
  through Cloudflare in the clear.
- **Even the note is health data if stored on the server.** Note A or B lets someone infer a
  health problem, and the CJEU reads health data broadly, including inference (Lindenapotheke
  C-21/23, 2024).
- So:
  - keep the note only on the device, if at all;
  - store on the server at most "questions answered on <date>, version N" (derived: this
    reveals nothing about health);
  - show the hard-day reminder to everyone in a generic form.
- **This conflicts with ADR-0046**, which is accepted and stores the answers in
  `readiness_answers` as consented health data. The B5 draft says the answers aren't stored.
  One of them has to give way, through a new ADR that supersedes 0046.

**(c) Liability.**

- An EU or German consumer app **cannot exclude or limit liability for death or personal
  injury caused by its own negligence**:
  - Directive 93/13 Annex 1(a);
  - BGB §309 Nr. 7a (void in standard terms, including slight negligence);
  - §309 Nr. 7b for gross negligence and intent on other damage.
- Tort liability (§823 BGB) exists anyway.
- From **9 Dec 2026** the new Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 makes software a
  product. Strict liability for defects causing injury then "cannot be limited or excluded by
  contract" [secondary].
- **What works:**
  - an honest description of the service ("general exercise suggestions; it doesn't examine
    you");
  - clear warnings and stop signs;
  - "you decide what you do".

  These are information, not exclusions. They also support contributory fault (§254 BGB,
  derived).
- **What is void:** "no liability for any injury", "at your own risk" used as an exclusion,
  "you waive all claims", "by continuing you confirm you are healthy".
- Wording to use, and to avoid: Evidence 2.3.

#### 3. "Can't be used outside Europe"

1. **Define it as the EU and EEA:** 27 EU countries plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway, and
   the EU territories that have their own country codes.
   - The full list of 37 codes: AT BE BG HR CY CZ DK EE FI FR DE GR IE IT LV LT LU MT NL PL PT
     RO SK SI ES SE, plus IS LI NO, plus AX GF GP MQ RE YT MF (derived; Greece is `GR`, not
     `EL`).
   - Reasons: GDPR, MDR and the Geo-blocking Regulation all apply across the EEA. The
     Geo-blocking Regulation was taken into the EEA Agreement in 2019, so refusing Norway while
     serving Germany risks breaching it (derived).
   - **Leave out the UK and Switzerland for now.** Each adds a local representative for
     health-data processing and its own device regime (from memory, not re-read). They can be
     added later.
   - **Never use the continent code.** Cloudflare's `EU` means Europe the continent, not the
     European Union. The brief says it includes Russia and Belarus; I couldn't confirm the list.
   - **The EU-membership field is no good either.** `ip.src.is_in_european_union` needs the
     Business plan, and it would leave out the EEA.
2. **Geo-blocking Regulation 2018/302 doesn't stop this.** It protects "customers": EU or EEA
   nationals or residents seeking a service "within the Union". It doesn't apply to people
   elsewhere. What it does forbid is serving only Germany, or refusing some EU or EEA
   countries.
3. **Enforce it in four layers.** Full detail is in Evidence 3.4.
   - **L1. Cloudflare WAF, one free custom rule** (Free allows 5). Block when
     `not ip.src.country in {…37 codes…}`, except the machine paths `/mcp`, `/oauth/token`,
     `/oauth/register` and `/.well-known/oauth-*`. Claude's MCP calls come from Anthropic's
     egress in the US (`160.79.104.0/21`, ADR-0054). Keep `/oauth/authorize` and `/connect`
     inside the rule, so connecting an AI needs a visit from Europe. Tor (`T1`) and unknown
     (`XX`) are blocked because they aren't on the list.
   - **L2. The app reads `CF-IPCountry`.** It trusts the header only from
     `TC_TRUSTED_PROXIES`, as it does `CF-Connecting-IP`. Browser routes outside the list get
     the app's own page, "Unavailable in your region", with status 451. This also covers
     sign-up and sign-in if the WAF rule is ever off.
   - **L3. Sign-up asks for the country of residence.** The list holds the 37 entries plus
     "Somewhere else", which is refused. The terms say the service is for people living in the
     EU or EEA. Store the country; it isn't health data.
   - **L4. A "last seen in Europe" date on the account,** set by any web request from the
     list. The Telegram bot and the MCP endpoint serve an account only if that date is within
     30 days (derived; the number is a choice). They can't see a location themselves: the bot
     uses long polling, so no IP reaches the app.
4. **A member who travels outside Europe:** the website shows the 451 page there. The bot and
   the connected AI keep working until 30 days after the last visit from Europe, then pause
   until the next one. Tell people to export before a long trip.
   - The simpler option, cutting off the bot and AI at once, isn't possible, because neither
     shows where the person is.
   - A VPN beats every layer. Cloudflare itself says IP location shouldn't be the only signal
     for compliance; the residence declaration and the terms are the other signals.

#### Things to decide (my suggestions in brackets)

1. ADR-0046 stores the answers, but B5 says they aren't stored. [Supersede 0046: browser-only
   answers, nothing on the server but the date and version.]
2. Part D condition-based adaptations in the research doc. [Recast them as neutral preferences,
   or drop them; they are the main medical-device risk.]
3. The UK and Switzerland. [Not at launch.]
4. The travel window for the bot and MCP. [30 days.]
5. Whether to ask PAR-Q+ for permission to show the German PAR-Q+ inside the app. [No; link out.]
6. A clinician to check the question and note wording, and a lawyer to check 2a, 2b and 2c.

  - ESC uses symptoms plus SCORE. The EAPC used an AHA/ACSM-type short questionnaire. DGSP
    relies on a physician's examination, and its lay form is the 2002 PAR-Q adaptation.
  - The gym sector in the UK and Germany uses the old PAR-Q.
  - PAR-Q+ is widely cited and translated, but I found no European body naming it as its
    standard.

#### 2. Law

**2.1 MDR and MDCG.**

- **MDCG 2019-11 rev.1** (June 2025). [part]: sections 3.1-3.3, the decision steps and the
  Rule 11 text.
  - "software only intended for non-medical purposes … such as … wellness or fitness apps, do
    not qualify as MDSW."
  - Qualification turns on the intended purpose "as described by the manufacturer". The
    intended purpose comes from "the label, … instructions for use or … promotional or sales
    materials or statements".
  - Decision step 4 asks whether the action is "for the benefit of individual patients". It
    excludes "generic diagnostic or treatment pathways (not directed to individual patients)".
  - Risk of harm "is not a criterion on whether the software qualifies".
  - Under Rule 11: "a device intended to prevent the risk of illnesses or pathologies by
    analysing physiological parameters … is in class IIa."
  - The medical purposes include "diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis,
    treatment or alleviation of disease".
  - Link:
    https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/b45335c5-1679-4c71-a91c-fc7a4d37f12b_en?filename=mdcg_2019_11_en.pdf
- **Borderline and Classification Manual v5** (2026): searched in full; it has no fitness or
  wellness app example. Link:
  https://health.ec.europa.eu/document/download/71a87df8-5ca1-4555-b453-b65bdf8de909_en?filename=md_borderline_manual_en.pdf
- **BfArM's orientation on medical apps:** health promotion, wellness and fitness are lifestyle;
  diagnosis, therapy and monitoring are medical [secondary, 2016-17 summaries].
- **Draft intended-purpose statement** (derived): "Training Coach is a general fitness app for
  adults. It suggests exercises and sessions from the person's own choices, preferences and
  logged training. It is not intended to diagnose, prevent, monitor, predict, treat or alleviate
  any disease or injury, and it does not assess anyone's health or whether they are fit to
  exercise. Its safety questions and stop signs repeat public health advice and suggest when to
  ask a doctor; the person decides."
- **What would tip it into a device** (derived from the above):
  - risk scores (SCORE2), "cleared" or "not cleared", or "your heart risk";
  - warnings drawn from heart-rate or band data that suggest illness;
  - programmes or exercise changes keyed to a declared disease (research doc Part D, items
    19-25), for example hiding lifts because of retinopathy, or hypoglycaemia prompts because of
    insulin;
  - claims such as "rehab" or "for arthritis".
- **What keeps it a fitness app:**
  - the same generic notes for everyone;
  - preferences without reasons;
  - the talk test as a neutral option;
  - stop signs as general public-health text (generic, not individual: decision step 4).

**2.2 GDPR.**

- **Art. 4(2)** defines processing broadly, including "collection … use, disclosure by
  transmission … erasure" (well known; I didn't re-read it). Sending the answers and dropping
  them is still processing, and health data needs Art. 9(2)(a) explicit consent. Here they would
  also pass through Cloudflare, which terminates TLS (derived from the setup).
- **Controller without access:** the CJEU in Wirtschaftsakademie C-210/16 and Jehovan todistajat
  C-25/17 [secondary]. Joint responsibility "does not require each of them to have access to the
  personal data". That leaves room to argue the UG is a controller even of browser-only
  processing, since it designs it.
- **The other view:** DIGITALEUROPE, in its response to the EDPB connected-vehicles guidelines
  1/2020, argued that purely local processing is outside the GDPR because "no controller or
  processor is actually processing the data" [secondary]. The EDPB itself prefers local
  processing and put purely personal in-car use under the household exemption [secondary].
- **Health data by inference:** Lindenapotheke C-21/23 (4 Oct 2024) holds that it is enough that
  health "can be inferred … by means of a mental combination or deduction" [secondary]. So a
  stored note A or B is health data, and so is a switch labelled "I take beta-blockers"
  (derived).
- **Practical design** (derived):
  - compute in the browser, hold the answers in memory only, and don't put them in a URL;
  - no analytics or error reporting that captures form state;
  - optionally keep the note number in the device's localStorage. That is "strictly necessary"
    storage for a service the person asked for, so it is exempt from the consent rule in
    §25(2) TDDDG (derived; not re-read).
  - the server keeps only the date and version.
  - Don't ask the questions over Telegram: that would send them to Telegram and to the server.
- Links: https://curia.europa.eu/jcms/upload/docs/application/pdf/2024-10/cp240159en.pdf (press
  release, not read); https://fpf.org/blog/edpb-draft-guidelines-on-connected-cars-focus-on-data-protection-by-design-and-push-for-consent/
  [secondary].

**2.3 Liability.**

- **Directive 93/13 Annex 1(a):** an indicatively unfair term is one "excluding or limiting the
  legal liability of a seller or supplier in the event of the death of a consumer" or personal
  injury caused by them. [full for that point]
  https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudr/1993/13/annex
- **BGB §309 Nr. 7a:** standard terms are void if they exclude or limit liability "für Schäden
  aus der Verletzung des Lebens, des Körpers oder der Gesundheit, die auf einer fahrlässigen
  Pflichtverletzung des Verwenders … beruhen". **7b** covers gross negligence for other damage.
  [full] https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/bgb/__309.html
- **PLD 2024/2853:**
  - software is a product "irrespective of how it is supplied", including SaaS;
  - it covers personal injury, including medically recognised psychological harm;
  - liability "cannot be limited or excluded by contract";
  - instructions are part of what makes a product defective or not, but warnings can't cure a
    defect;
  - it applies to products placed on the market after 9 Dec 2026;
  - free and open-source software supplied outside commercial activity is excluded.

  [secondary: CMS] https://cms.law/en/int/publication/the-new-eu-product-liability-directive-eu-2024-2853
- **Acceptable wording** (derived; for the lawyer):
  - "Training Coach suggests exercises. It can't see you or examine you, and it doesn't know
    your health. You decide what you do, how hard, and when to stop."
  - "If you have a health condition, symptoms, or doubts, ask a doctor before you start or
    before hard sessions."
  - "Stop and get help if… (stop signs). Call 112 if it doesn't settle."
  - "These settings are your preferences. We don't ask why, and they don't tell us anything
    about your health."
  - A German-style liability clause in the terms: unlimited for intent, gross negligence, and
    injury to life, body or health; for slight negligence otherwise, only for essential
    obligations, capped at typical foreseeable damage. This is the usual form that survives
    §§307 and 309; I didn't re-read the case law.
- **Void or risky wording:**
  - "We accept no liability for injury."
  - "You use the app entirely at your own risk" (as an exclusion).
  - "You release the UG from all claims."
  - "By continuing you confirm you are healthy, or have seen a doctor."
  - "Medically cleared", or any "clearance" (this also invites the MDR).
- DGSP's own 2002 form disclaims responsibility. That is a professional body's information
  sheet, not consumer terms, so it is no precedent for the app (derived).

**3.3 Geo-blocking Regulation 2018/302.** [full, Articles 1-4 as adopted]

- "customer" means "a consumer who is a national of, or has his or her place of residence in, a
  Member State … and receives a service … or seeks to do so, within the Union".
- Art. 3(1): no blocking "for reasons related to the customer's nationality, place of residence
  or place of establishment".
- Art. 1(2): purely internal situations are excluded.
- Art. 4(4): VAT-exempt small traders are excluded from 4(1)(b) only.
- Therefore (derived):
  - blocking people outside the EU or EEA is outside its scope;
  - serving only some member states is not;
  - a resident travelling abroad isn't "seeking … within the Union".
- "Service" means one "normally provided for remuneration" (Art. 2(17)). If the app stays free,
  whether the Regulation applies at all is a lawyer's question.
- EEA: Joint Committee Decision 311/2019 took it into the EEA Agreement [secondary, via search].
